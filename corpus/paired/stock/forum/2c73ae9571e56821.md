## Diagnosing Route Flapping Between Upstream ISPs: A Deep Dive

**Question:** I have a multihomed network using two upstream ISPs. We're experiencing BGP route flapping – prefixes are constantly being withdrawn and re-advertised between them. This is impacting stability. How can I diagnose the root cause, interpret the statistics, and prevent further disruption?

**Answer (Community Wiki - Please Add Your Experience):**

Route flapping is a frustrating problem. It’s not just about prefix instability; it can trigger cascading issues downstream and degrade user experience significantly. Here's a breakdown of how to tackle this, especially when dealing with multiple upstream ISPs.  This isn't exhaustive – BGP troubleshooting *can* be complex!

**1. Initial Observation & Data Collection:**

Before diving into tools, establish a baseline:

*   **Which prefixes are flapping?** Are they specific networks (e.g., only your customer routes) or broader transit announcements? This helps narrow the scope.
*   **Time correlation:** Does the flapping coincide with any network changes on *your* side or known events at either ISP?  Look for patterns.
*   **Severity:** How much disruption is this causing downstream? Is it just a minor blip, or are customers experiencing significant outages?

**2. Understanding BGP Statistics – Your Key Indicators:**

Most routers provide statistics related to BGP peer sessions and route changes. These are your first clues:

*   **`show bgp summary` (or equivalent):** This displays the status of each BGP session with your ISPs. Look for states beyond "Established."  "Active," "Connect," or repeated transitions between "Idle" and "Connected" indicate connectivity problems *at the TCP level*.  This isn’t necessarily route flapping, but it's a prerequisite to it.
*   **`show bgp neighbor <neighbor_ip> statistics` (or equivalent):** This is crucial! Key metrics include:
    *   **Restart Count:** Number of times the BGP session has been reset.  High values point to underlying connectivity or configuration issues.
    *   **In/Out Updates:** Shows the number of routing updates received and sent. Significant disparities *could* indicate filtering problems, but are less often a direct cause of flapping itself.
    *   **Keepalive/Hold Time:** These should be consistent between peers.  Mismatches can lead to session resets.
    *   **Route Refresh Count:** Frequent route refreshes suggest instability or a lack of convergence.
    *   **Flaps (or Route Change Events):** *This is what you're directly looking for*. This metric indicates the number of times a BGP route has been withdrawn and re-advertised.  A flapping prefix will have a high "Flaps" count.

**3. Route Dampening: Friend or Foe?**

Route dampening aims to mitigate flapping by penalizing routes that experience frequent changes. It works by assigning penalties to withdrawing prefixes, eventually reducing their BGP attribute (usually MED) and making them less preferred.

*   **When it helps:** Route dampening *can* be effective when dealing with minor, transient instability caused by misconfigured peers or temporary routing anomalies.  It prevents these from propagating downstream too aggressively.
*   **When it masks problems:** **This is the critical point.** Dampening *hides* the underlying cause! If you have a persistent problem (e.g., faulty hardware on an ISP's side, a misconfiguration in their routing policy), dampening will only suppress the symptoms, not solve the root issue.  It can also lead to sub-optimal routing if routes are unfairly penalized.
*   **Best Practice:** Use route dampening cautiously and with *low* penalty values initially. **Always investigate the cause of flapping before relying heavily on dampening.**

**4. Tracing Flapping Back to a Specific Peer Link – The Investigation Phase:**

This is where it gets more involved. Here’s a methodology:

*   **Isolate the Problem:** If *all* prefixes are flapping, it's likely a broader issue (e.g., your router configuration).  If only specific prefixes are affected, focus on those routes and their relationship to each ISP.
*   **Correlation with Events:** Review logs from both ISPs (if possible) around the time of the flaps. Did either peer experience connectivity issues or routing policy changes?
*   **Traffic Mirroring/Packet Capture:**  This is powerful but requires careful planning.  Mirror traffic between your router and each ISP's peer link. Analyze the BGP update messages to see which peer is initiating the withdrawals/re-advertisements. Tools like Wireshark are essential here.
*   **BGP Debugging (Router Dependent):** Many routers have debugging features that can capture raw BGP messages, providing detailed information about what's happening during route exchanges.  Use with caution as this can generate *a lot* of data.
* **Check for AS Path Prepending:** Is one ISP adding a significantly longer AS path than the other? This could be influencing routing decisions and causing instability.

**5. Specific Considerations with Multihoming:**

*   **Tie-breaker Policies:** Carefully review your tie-breaker policies (e.g., local preference, MED). Inconsistent or overly aggressive policies can exacerbate flapping.
*   **Load Balancing vs. Preemption:** Are you truly load balancing, or are prefixes being preempted frequently? Preemption is a form of instability.  Consider using BGP attributes to influence routing decisions more strategically.

**Important Notes:**

*   **ISP Communication:**  Open communication with your ISPs is vital! They may have insights into their network’s behavior that you don't.
*   **Documentation:** Thoroughly document all changes and findings. This helps in future troubleshooting efforts.
