## Diagnosing Persistent Route Flapping Between Upstream ISPs: Beyond the Basics

**Question:** We have a multihomed network using two upstream ISPs. We're seeing significant route flapping on several prefixes from both peers, causing instability for our customers and driving up monitoring alerts. How can we systematically diagnose this issue? We’ve already checked basic interface connectivity and verified that there are no obvious configuration errors.

**Answer:** Okay, persistent route flapping is *extremely* frustrating and usually points to more than just a simple mistake. It’s rarely as easy as “ISP A has a transient failure.” Let's break down how to diagnose it methodically.

**1. Immediate Mitigation & Monitoring - The Baseline**

Before digging in, take these steps to minimize impact:

*   **Implement Basic Route Dampening:**  Often overlooked, this can *temporarily* provide stability while you investigate (more on its pros/cons later).
*   **Enable Detailed Logging:** Critical! Configure your router (Cisco, Juniper, etc.) for comprehensive BGP logging.  The specifics will vary by vendor. Here are some examples:

    *   **Cisco:** `logging debug ip bgp *` (use with caution on production – this generates a *lot* of logs). Consider filtering to specific peers/prefixes.
    *   **Juniper:** Set detailed logging levels for the affected BGP sessions and prefixes via `/optics/configure`.

*   **Monitor Route Statistics:** Most routers provide per-prefix statistics (flap counts, withdrawal counts, etc.).  Access these from the command line or through SNMP monitoring tools. These are your primary diagnostic data points. For example:
    * **Cisco:** `show ip bgp <prefix>`
    * **Juniper:** `show route protocol bgp <prefix>`

**2. Understanding Route Flap Statistics – What are you looking for?**

These statistics provide vital clues. Look at the following *in conjunction*:

*   **Withdrawals:** Frequent withdrawals (routes being pulled) indicate instability on one or both sides.
*   **Updates:**  Frequent updates (routes being announced/modified) also show flapping.
*   **Bestpath Changes:** These events record when your router considers a different path “best” and switches routing tables accordingly. *Significant* numbers of these are a primary indicator of flapping.
*   **Last Change Notification Time:** The timestamp for the most recent route announcement or withdrawal.

**3. Is Route Dampening Helping…or Hiding Something?**

Route dampening (typically using `maximum-routes` and `route-refresh interval`) stabilizes routes by penalizing prefixes experiencing frequent changes. *However*, it can mask root causes, delaying resolution:

*   **When it’s useful:** If the flapping is due to minor, transient instability on an ISP's network, dampening *can* smooth things out (though it doesn't solve the underlying problem).
*   **When it masks:** When there are genuine routing policy errors or deeper infrastructure problems within one of your ISPs, route-dampening will simply suppress those issues temporarily. The flapping may return after a period. It can also cause suboptimal routes to stay stuck if they happen to be stable for a while.

Therefore, use dampening with caution and *always* investigate the underlying reason when it kicks in. Route statistics are key here – if you’re consistently seeing withdrawals/updates even with dampened routes, there is a serious issue that needs immediate attention.

**4. Tracing the Flapping to a Specific Peer Link - The Investigation**

Now for the hard part: figuring out *which* peer and (ideally) *where* along their network the flapping originates.

*   **Correlation:**  Do both ISPs experience identical flapping patterns on prefixes? If so, it’s more likely an issue triggered by a common transit provider they both use or something affecting your router itself.
*   **Peer-Specific Analysis:** Focus on statistics *per peer*. Identify which peer consistently has higher withdrawal/update counts. This isolates the problem to that upstream link.
*   **Ping Loops and Path Diversity (Highly Recommended):**  If you suspect a common transit issue, temporarily change your policies to test different paths between your router and each ISP, even if it increases cost briefly. Does this affect what gets announced?
*   **Extended Logging:** If the flapping is peer-specific, enable *extended* logging for that BGP session (as much detail as your vendor allows). Examine these logs closely for patterns.  Look for anything unusual related to AS path changes or neighbor flaps.
*   **TCP Dump/Packet Capture (Advanced):** As a last resort (and only if you are comfortable), perform TCP dumps on the links between your router and each ISP. Analyze these captures using Wireshark or similar tools. This can reveal problems like corrupted packets, timing issues, or unexpected session resets.

**5. Considerations for Multihoming Setup & Policies:**

* **Default Local Preference (DPL):** Make sure you have properly configured local preference to accurately reflect your policy and route selection goals. Incorrect policies here will cause flapping because of the router constantly choosing between paths that are equally good or bad.
*   **BGP Communities:** Coordinate community usage with your ISPs – routing loops can easily occur if communities aren't handled correctly.
* **AS Path Prepending:**  If you use AS path prepending, ensure it isn’t inadvertently creating instability by making a route appear less desirable during transient network issues.

**Example Scenario & Troubleshooting:**

Let's say ISP-A consistently has higher withdrawal counts for the `/24` prefix. You review logs and see repeated announcements of this route followed quickly by withdrawals along with "AS Path length exceeded" notices. This strongly suggests that a faulty transit AS in ISP-A’s network is sending corrupted or incorrect routing information, forcing your router to withdraw then re-announce the route repeatedly.

**Final Thoughts:** Route flapping resolution often involves significant detective work and communication with upstream ISPs.  Detailed monitoring, methodical analysis of statistics, coupled with focused debugging efforts are crucial for identifying - and resolving - those difficult issues.
