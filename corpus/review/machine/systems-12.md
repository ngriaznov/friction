The SPSC queue looks correct to me on the algorithm — separate `head` (consumer-owned) and `tail` (producer-owned) atomic indices into a fixed ring, producer only ever writes `tail` and reads `head`, consumer only ever writes `head` and reads `tail`, which is exactly the single-writer-per-index discipline that makes SPSC easier to get right than MPMC. A couple of things worth polishing.

The ordering choices are close but not quite optimal. The producer's store to `tail` after writing the slot uses `memory_order_release`, correct. But its load of `head` (to check whether there's room) uses `memory_order_acquire` when `memory_order_relaxed` would suffice and be cheaper — the producer doesn't need to synchronize-with anything the consumer wrote before advancing `head`, it only needs the numeric value to know how much room exists. Same on the consumer side in reverse: its load of `tail` to check for available items should be `acquire` (correct, and you have it right there) but its store to `head` after consuming should be `release`, which you also have right. So really it's just the one over-strict acquire on the producer's `head` load — small, but on architectures where acquire loads aren't free (mostly non-x86) this is a real, if minor, cost on every single push.

Second, `head` and `tail` are declared as separate `std::atomic<size_t>` member variables with no cache-line separation between them. For SPSC specifically this matters even more than for MPSC in some ways, since you have exactly two threads pounding on two adjacent atomics continuously — put `alignas(64)` on each (or wrap in a small padded struct) to stop them sharing a cache line and ping-ponging between the producer's and consumer's cores.

Nothing here is a correctness bug — I'd approve this as-is if it's not on a hot enough path to care about the ordering/padding overhead — but if this queue is meant to be a genuine low-latency SPSC channel, I'd fix both before merging, since they're each a few characters of change for a real measurable win on the intended use case.
