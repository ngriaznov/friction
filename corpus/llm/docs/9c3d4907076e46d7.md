# Reading a Soil-Moisture Sensor with SoilSense

This tutorial walks through wiring a capacitive soil-moisture sensor to an Arduino-compatible board, reading it with the SoilSense library, and calibrating it so the numbers mean something. Budget about an hour, most of which is waiting for soil to dry out during calibration.

## What you need

- An Arduino-compatible board with at least one analog input (Uno, Nano, and most ESP32 boards all work)
- A capacitive soil-moisture sensor — the flat fiberglass kind, not the two-prong resistive probe, which corrodes within a few weeks
- Three jumper wires
- A pot of soil you don't mind soaking

Resistive probes will technically work with this library, but their readings drift as the electrodes oxidize, and recalibrating every fortnight gets old.

## Wiring

The sensor has three pins, usually labeled on the silkscreen. Connect them like this:

- **VCC** on the sensor to the **5V** pin on the board. If you're on a 3.3V board like an ESP32, use 3.3V instead — the sensor tolerates both, but the output range scales with supply voltage, so pick one and stick with it through calibration.
- **GND** on the sensor to any **GND** pin on the board.
- **AOUT** on the sensor to **A0**.

Keep the sensor's exposed electronics above the soil line. Most boards have a printed maximum-insertion mark; push past it and moisture will eventually find its way into the trace and give you nonsense readings.

## The sketch

Install SoilSense through the Library Manager, or drop the folder into `~/Arduino/libraries` if you're working from a zip.

```cpp
#include <SoilSense.h>

SoilSense sensor(A0);

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(9600);
  sensor.begin();
  sensor.setRange(310, 640);  // wet, dry — replace after calibration
}

void loop() {
  int raw = sensor.readRaw();
  float pct = sensor.readPercent();

  Serial.print("raw=");
  Serial.print(raw);
  Serial.print("  moisture=");
  Serial.print(pct, 1);
  Serial.println("%");

  delay(2000);
}
```

Two methods matter here. `readRaw()` gives you the ADC value straight from the pin, 0–1023 on an Uno. `readPercent()` maps that value onto the range you passed to `setRange()` and returns 0–100. Note the argument order: **wet first, dry second**. Capacitive sensors read *lower* when wet, which trips up nearly everyone the first time.

`readPercent()` also clamps its output, so a reading slightly outside your calibrated range comes back as 0.0 or 100.0 rather than a negative number.

## Calibration

The default range in the example above is a placeholder. Your sensor, your soil, and your supply voltage all shift the numbers, so measure your own.

**Step 1 — dry reading.** Hold the sensor in open air, or push it into soil you've let dry out completely. Watch the serial monitor until `raw` settles, which takes ten or fifteen seconds. Write the number down. On a 5V Uno with a typical capacitive board, expect something in the low 600s.

**Step 2 — wet reading.** Submerge the sensor in a glass of water up to the insertion line. Wait for the value to settle again and record it. Expect roughly 300.

**Step 3 — apply the values.** Change `setRange()` to your two numbers, wet first:

```cpp
sensor.setRange(298, 617);
```

Reflash and confirm that air reads near 0% and water reads near 100%.

**Step 4 — sanity-check in soil.** Water a pot thoroughly, let it drain for twenty minutes, and take a reading. Well-watered potting mix usually lands somewhere between 55% and 75%. If you're getting 100%, the pot hasn't drained yet.

A note on repeatability: don't recalibrate in one soil type and deploy in another. Sandy mix and dense compost hold water differently enough that the same reading means different things. If you're moving the sensor between pots, redo step 4 for each one and note what "watered" looks like there.

Once the numbers are stable, you have a real measurement to trigger a pump, log to a file, or just print to a display.
