# MoltenPath Troubleshooting Guide

This guide covers three problems MoltenPath users report most often: layer shifting mid-print, nozzle clogs, and bed adhesion failures. Work through the diagnostic steps in order — each section starts with the cheapest check and escalates toward hardware fixes.

## Layer Shifting Mid-Print

Layer shifting shows up as a sudden horizontal offset in otherwise clean layers, usually on one axis only. It is almost always mechanical, not a firmware bug, but MoltenPath's motion log (`Menu > Diagnostics > Motion Log`) will tell you which axis lost steps and at what print time, which narrows the search fast.

**Diagnose:**
1. Open the motion log after a failed print and note whether the shift is on X, Y, or both. A single-axis shift points to that axis's belt or motor; both axes shifting together usually means a frame or power issue.
2. Check belt tension by pinching the belt at its midpoint. It should deflect slightly, like a guitar string tuned low — not slack, not drum-tight.
3. Inspect the pulley set screws on the stepper shaft. A pulley that spins on the shaft without moving the belt is a top cause of shifting and is easy to miss on a visual check alone.
4. Confirm the stepper current in `Settings > Motion > Current` matches your motor's rated current. MoltenPath ships conservative defaults that undercurrent some third-party motors.

**Fix:** Re-tension the belt, tighten pulley set screws (add a flat spot to the shaft with a small file if they keep backing out), and raise current in increments of 50 mA, reprinting a calibration tower between changes. If shifting persists at correct tension and current, check for a loose gantry rod or a print speed that exceeds your acceleration settings under `Settings > Motion > Jerk/Acceleration` — lower jerk before lowering speed.

## Nozzle Clogs

A clog presents as thin under-extrusion that degrades into no extrusion at all, often with a clicking extruder motor as it skips over blocked filament.

**Diagnose:**
1. Run `Menu > Maintenance > Extrude Test` at printing temperature. No filament output, or output far thinner than expected, confirms a clog rather than a slicer or model issue.
2. Note whether the clog appeared after a filament swap or a long idle period at temperature — both are classic causes of heat creep and carbonized residue at the nozzle tip.

**Fix:** Start with a cold pull: heat to the filament's printing temperature, let a fresh length of filament sit for thirty seconds, then drop to 90°C and pull firmly once the extruder resists — the debris comes out with it. Repeat until the pulled strand is clean. If cold pulls don't clear it, remove the nozzle and run a needle of the correct gauge through it while hot, or replace the nozzle outright if it shows scoring. Persistent recurring clogs usually mean your retraction settings are too aggressive for the hotend's melt zone, or the cooling fan is blowing on the heat break — check the fan shroud alignment.

## Bed Adhesion Failures

Adhesion failure shows as corners lifting, a print detaching mid-job, or a first layer that never quite bonds.

**Diagnose:**
1. Run `Menu > Calibration > Bed Mesh` and view the height map. Uneven mesh values across the bed indicate a leveling problem rather than a temperature or surface problem.
2. Check first-layer squish visually: filament should look slightly flattened, not round (too high) or scraped and inconsistent (too low).
3. Confirm bed temperature reaches its setpoint before printing starts — a bed that's still climbing when the nozzle begins moving is a common cause of edge lifting on larger prints.

**Fix:** Re-run bed leveling and, if the mesh is still uneven after leveling, check the bed springs or clips for wear. Clean the print surface with isopropyl alcohol between prints — oils from handling are enough to prevent bonding on PEI and glass alike. For persistently warping materials like ABS, enable the enclosure preheat option under `Settings > Enclosure` if your printer has one, and raise bed temperature by 5°C before touching first-layer flow rate.
