They're two different physical events that happen to sound similar, and the gap between them is where nearly all of your control over the final flavour lives.

**First crack** is steam. Green coffee comes in around 10–12% moisture. As the bean heats past the boiling point of the water trapped inside, pressure builds until the cell structure gives way and the bean fractures audibly and roughly doubles in size. The sound is sharp and distinct — most people compare it to popcorn. It's loud enough that you'll hear it over a fluid-bed roaster's fan, and it arrives as a rolling wave rather than a single pop: a few beans, then many, then it tapers off over 60–90 seconds.

**Second crack** is structural, not steam-driven. By then the free water is long gone and the bean's cellulose matrix is breaking down under continued heat while CO₂ from ongoing pyrolysis builds up inside. The sound is quieter, faster, and more brittle — closer to Rice Krispies in milk than to popcorn. Oils start migrating to the surface around here, which is why very dark roasts look shiny.

**Timing.** On a typical home drum or air roaster running a normal profile, second crack starts roughly **3 to 5 minutes** after first crack begins. Bean temperature is somewhere near 196–205 °C at first crack and 224–230 °C at second, though absolute numbers off a home roaster's probe are close to meaningless — probe placement varies wildly between machines, so trust the sound and your own logged times, not someone else's temperature target. That gap compresses badly if you're driving hard: dump a lot of heat in after first crack and you can get to second in 90 seconds, which usually produces a roast that tastes simultaneously underdeveloped and burnt.

**What stopping at different points does:**

- **Just at the end of first crack.** Light roast. Highest acidity, most of the origin character still present — the florals in an Ethiopian, the fruit in a washed Kenyan. Also where underdevelopment hides: if you drop too early you get grassy, sour, papery notes that people mistake for "bright."
- **~1 minute after first crack ends.** Light-medium. Acidity still leads but has rounded out, more sweetness, more body. This is where a lot of specialty filter roasts land.
- **Halfway to second crack.** Medium. Origin character is fading and roast character — caramel, nutty, chocolate — is coming forward. The balance point.
- **Right at the onset of second crack.** Medium-dark. Origin character mostly gone. Bittersweet chocolate, some smokiness starting. Common for espresso blends that want body over clarity.
- **Into rolling second crack.** Dark. You're now tasting roast, not bean. Carbony, oily surface, thin acidity. Whatever you paid for the green coffee's terroir, you've burned it off.

A useful way to think about it: **development time ratio**, the portion of total roast time spent after first crack starts, is commonly kept somewhere in the 18–25% range. Log first crack time and drop time on every roast, taste, and adjust. That log will teach you more about your machine than any temperature chart.
