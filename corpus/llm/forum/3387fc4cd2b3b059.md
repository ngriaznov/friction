Route dampening is where most people start with this, and it's usually the wrong first move. Dampening doesn't tell you anything about *why* prefixes are flapping — it just stops you from hearing about it. Find the cause first, then decide whether dampening is appropriate.

## First: is it your session, or their prefixes?

These are very different problems and the fix is different, so separate them before anything else.

**Session flapping** means the BGP adjacency itself is bouncing — every prefix from that peer disappears and reappears together. Check uptime on both sessions:

```
show bgp summary
```

Look at the `Up/Down` column. If a peer shows an uptime of a few minutes when you've been up for weeks, that session is resetting. Then:

```
show log messages | match BGP
show bgp neighbor 203.0.113.1 | match "reset|last error|notification"
```

The reset reason matters enormously. `Hold Timer Expired` points at the transport — packet loss, CPU starvation on either end, or an MTU problem. `Cease/Maximum Number of Prefixes Reached` is a config issue, not a network one. `Administratively Reset` means somebody on the other side is doing maintenance and didn't tell you.

**Prefix flapping** means the session is stable but individual routes are withdrawn and readvertised. Uptime on the peer will be long while the RIB churns underneath it.

## Reading flap statistics

Assuming you have dampening configured (even just to observe), you get per-prefix flap counts:

```
show bgp flap-statistics
show ip bgp dampened-paths
show ip bgp flap-statistics regexp _64500$
```

You're looking at three things per prefix: flap count, penalty value, and the reuse time. A prefix with a flap count of 4 and a decaying penalty is noise. A prefix at 30+ flaps with a penalty pinned near the suppress limit is a real, ongoing problem.

The pattern in that output is the actual diagnostic signal:

- **Flaps spread across many origin ASNs, arriving through one peer** — the problem is somewhere in that upstream's network or beyond it, not at your edge. You're a spectator.
- **Flaps concentrated in one origin AS or one customer block** — that's a downstream problem, possibly one of your own customers with a bouncing link.
- **Every prefix from a peer flapping in lockstep** — that's session flapping wearing a disguise. Go back to the previous section.

## Tracing it to a physical link

If session flapping is confirmed, correlate BGP resets against interface and transport state. Timestamps do most of the work here:

```
show interfaces ge-0/0/1 extensive | match "error|drop|CRC|flap"
show interfaces ge-0/0/1 | match "Last flapped"
```

Rising CRC or input errors on a circuit facing an ISP handoff usually means optics, a dirty connector, or a bad patch in the middle-mile. If the interface is clean but BGP still drops, suspect something between you and the peer — many handoffs traverse a carrier's L2 transport with its own failure modes that never show on your port.

Two specific things worth ruling out early:

1. **MTU mismatch.** Sessions establish fine, then die when a large UPDATE needs to be sent. Test with `ping 203.0.113.1 size 1472 do-not-fragment` and compare against the negotiated MTU. This one is sneaky because it looks time-correlated with route churn rather than with the link.
2. **BFD timers that are too aggressive.** If you've got BFD at 50ms intervals over a circuit with occasional microbursts, you'll tear down a perfectly serviceable session. Relax to 300ms x 3 and see whether the flapping stops. If it does, the BFD config was the bug.

Also check the control plane on your own box: `show system processes extensive` during a flap window. A router pegged at 100% CPU will miss keepalives and blame the peer.

## When dampening actually helps

Dampening is right when you're receiving churn you can't fix and can't afford to process — a distant origin AS flapping a prefix hundreds of times a day, chewing your CPU on repeated best-path computations. That's what it was designed for.

It's wrong when the flapping is on your own edge or your customers'. Suppressing a customer prefix for an hour because their link bounced three times converts a 20-second outage into a 60-minute one. RFC 7196 half-lives (suppress 6000, half-life 15 min, max-suppress 60 min) are far gentler than the old RIPE-229 defaults, so if you're running something inherited from 2003, update it.

Practical middle ground: enable dampening in monitor-only form on transit-received routes, leave it off for customer-originated prefixes, and treat the flap-statistics table as a report you read weekly rather than an enforcement mechanism.
