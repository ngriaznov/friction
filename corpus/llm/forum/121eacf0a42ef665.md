The thing that makes 3-way switches confusing is that they have no ON and OFF. A 3-way is a single-pole double-throw switch: it connects one terminal to one of two others, always. Which position is "on" depends entirely on where the other switch is sitting. Once you stop looking for an off position, the wiring makes sense.

**Kill the breaker and verify with a non-contact tester at the box before you touch anything.** Also worth saying up front: this is a common DIY job in most jurisdictions, but permit and inspection rules vary, and if any of the wiring you uncover looks like it predates grounded cable, get an electrician in rather than working around it.

## Identifying the terminals

Each 3-way switch has three current-carrying screws plus a green ground screw:

- **One common terminal.** It is a different color from the other two — black, dark bronze, or occasionally copper-colored, and on most switches it's marked "COMMON" on the back of the body. It sits by itself, usually at one corner.
- **Two traveler terminals.** Both brass, both the same color as each other. They are interchangeable — you can swap the two travelers and nothing changes.

If you can't tell which is which by color, put a continuity tester between one screw and each of the others while flipping the toggle. The screw that reads continuity to a different partner in each toggle position is the common.

## The standard layout (power at the first switch)

This is the most common arrangement:

1. **Switch box 1:** the incoming hot from the panel lands on the **common** of switch 1.
2. **Between the boxes:** a 3-conductor cable (14/3 or 12/3 to match the circuit). The black and red both run to **traveler** terminals — black to a brass screw on switch 1, black to a brass screw on switch 2, same for red. Order doesn't matter as long as each conductor lands on a traveler at both ends.
3. **Switch box 2:** the **common** of switch 2 goes out to the light fixture's hot.
4. **Neutrals** are spliced straight through in both boxes. They never land on a switch. (Note that NEC 2011 onward requires a neutral in the switch box, so if you're pulling new cable, run it even if this switch doesn't need it — the next smart switch will.)
5. **Grounds** to the green screw on each switch and spliced through.

## The mistake that reverses the logic

By far the most common error is landing the incoming hot on a **traveler** and one of the travelers on the **common**.

The symptom is distinctive: the light works from one switch some of the time, and the other switch appears dead in one position. You'll flip switch A and get nothing, flip switch B, then switch A starts working again — but only until you move B back. People usually diagnose this as a bad switch and replace it, which changes nothing.

If you see that behavior, the fix is almost always at the box where the hot enters. Pull the switch, confirm which screw is the odd-colored one, and make sure the feed is on it — not on brass.
