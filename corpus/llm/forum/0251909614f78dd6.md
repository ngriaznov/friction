A 1.092 OG imperial stout sitting at 1.038 after eight days isn't mysterious — it's almost always a pitch rate or oxygen problem, and everything else people suggest is downstream of those two. Let me go through the suspects in the order I'd actually check them.

**Pitch rate.** One pack of liquid yeast into 5 gallons of 1.092 wort is roughly a third of what you need. The standard target for an ale is about 0.75 million cells per mL per degree Plato; at 22 °P that's around 310 billion cells for a 19 L batch. A fresh liquid pack is 100 billion at best, and it's rarely fresh. Dry yeast is denser — two 11 g packs gets you close — but if you pitched a single pack of anything, the yeast spent its limited reserves reproducing instead of fermenting, ran out of steam around 60% attenuation, and flocculated. That timing matches what you're describing exactly.

**Oxygenation.** High-gravity wort holds less dissolved oxygen than a normal-gravity wort at the same temperature, and yeast need O2 to build the sterols and unsaturated fatty acids that make healthy cell membranes. Shaking the carboy for 30 seconds gets you maybe 4 ppm; you want 10–12 ppm for a beer this size, which realistically means pure O2 through a stone for 60–90 seconds. Under-oxygenated yeast produce a first generation with weak membranes that can't tolerate the rising alcohol, and they quit early. This is the second most common cause and it compounds the first.

**Temperature.** Less likely to be the primary cause, but worth ruling out. If ambient dropped and the beer followed it down to the mid-60s F while the yeast was still working, they'll flocculate and sit. High-gravity ferments also generate real heat early, so a beer that started at 66 °F may have peaked at 74 °F and stressed the yeast before crashing. Check where the fermenter actually is, not what the room thermostat says.

**So: diacetyl rest or new starter?**

They solve different problems and you're describing the second one.

A diacetyl rest — raising to about 68–70 °F for a couple of days — tells yeast that are *still in suspension and still active* to reabsorb the diacetyl they produced. It's a cleanup step, not a restart. That said, warming the fermenter and gently rousing the yeast back into suspension costs you nothing and is genuinely worth trying first. Swirl the carboy, bring it up to 70 °F, give it 48 hours. If gravity moves at all, you had a flocculation problem and it will finish on its own.

If it doesn't move, pitch a fresh, actively fermenting starter. Two important details: make the starter at normal gravity (around 1.040) so the yeast are healthy going in, and pitch it at high krausen rather than crashing and decanting. Pitching dormant yeast into a 9% ABV environment is asking them to do the hardest possible job on an empty tank. Some people prefer to step it up by blending some of the stalled beer into the starter first, which is more work but gentler.

A dry high-alcohol strain rehydrated properly also works well here and is less fuss.

Next time: build a starter sized for the gravity, oxygenate with pure O2, and consider adding half the fermentables 24 hours in rather than all up front. Your beer is very likely salvageable — 1.038 stouts are drinkable, just sweet.
