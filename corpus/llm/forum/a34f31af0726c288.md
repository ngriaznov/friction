The short version: if you run enough tests, you will find "significant" results even when nothing is going on. p-hacking is what happens when the number of tests you ran isn't reflected in the threshold you judge them against — sometimes deliberately, more often through a series of individually reasonable decisions.

**Where the false positives come from**

A p-value under the null hypothesis is uniformly distributed on [0, 1]. That's the whole trick. If the null is true, your p-value is essentially a random draw from a uniform distribution, and the probability it lands below 0.05 is exactly 5%. That 5% *is* your Type I error rate — it's not a bug, it's the rate you agreed to when you picked alpha.

The problem is that 5% is a per-test rate, and people report it as though it were a per-study rate.

**The worked example**

Suppose you test 20 independent hypotheses at alpha = 0.05, and suppose — this is the important part — that all 20 nulls are true. Nothing in your data is real. What's the chance you report at least one significant finding?

P(at least one significant) = 1 − P(none significant)
= 1 − (1 − 0.05)^20
= 1 − 0.95^20
= 1 − 0.3585
≈ **0.6415**

So there's a 64% chance you find at least one "significant" effect in a dataset where every effect is exactly zero. The expected number of false positives is 20 × 0.05 = 1. On average, one of your twenty hypotheses gets a gold star for no reason at all.

Push it further and it gets worse fast. At 100 tests: 1 − 0.95^100 ≈ 99.4%, with an expected 5 false positives. This is why "we looked at 40 subgroups and found that the drug works in left-handed women over 60" should make you deeply suspicious.

**The Bonferroni correction**

The simplest fix is to divide alpha by the number of tests:

alpha_adjusted = alpha / m = 0.05 / 20 = **0.0025**

Now a result needs p < 0.0025 to count. Check what this buys you:

1 − (1 − 0.0025)^20 = 1 − 0.9975^20 ≈ 0.0488

Just under 5% — which is the point. Bonferroni controls the *family-wise error rate* (FWER): the probability of making even one false positive across the entire set of tests is now held at roughly your original alpha.

Equivalently, you can leave the threshold at 0.05 and multiply each p-value by m instead (capping at 1). Same decisions, and it's easier to report.

**The cost, and the alternative**

Bonferroni is conservative. Controlling FWER means you lose power — real effects that would have shown up at p = 0.01 now get thrown out. With 20 tests that's tolerable. With 20,000 (genomics, neuroimaging), it's brutal, and you'll reject almost everything true along with everything false.

That's why those fields usually control the *false discovery rate* instead, typically with Benjamini–Hochberg. FDR asks a different question: not "what's the chance I made any mistake?" but "what fraction of my declared discoveries are wrong?" Allowing 5% of your significant findings to be false is a much weaker demand than allowing a 5% chance of a single false finding anywhere, and it gives you far more power. For exploratory work where the findings will be validated later anyway, that's usually the right trade.

**The part corrections don't fix**

Multiple-comparison corrections only work if you know *m*. The insidious form of p-hacking is the kind where you never counted:

- You tried three outlier-exclusion rules and reported the one that worked.
- You collected data, checked p, and collected more because it was "close."
- You had five plausible outcome measures and the paper describes one.
- You added a covariate, then a different covariate.

Each of those is a hidden test. Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn's "researcher degrees of freedom" work showed that a handful of these choices, made in good faith, can push the real false-positive rate over 60% while every individual p-value in the paper looks fine.

No correction can save you here, because you can't divide by a number you never wrote down. The fix is procedural rather than statistical: preregister your analysis, report every test you ran including the ones that failed, and be honest about which analyses were planned and which were exploration. Exploration is legitimate — it just doesn't get to be labeled confirmation.
