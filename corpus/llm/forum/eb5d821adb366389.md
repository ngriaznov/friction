**Re: Rear hub feels gritty — worth rebuilding or replace the wheel?**

Rebuild it. Cup-and-cone hubs are one of the few things on a bike that genuinely reward home service, and a wheel that's true and tensioned properly will outlast a cheap replacement by years. Here's the whole process as I do it. Took me about three hours the first time, maybe forty minutes now.

**Tools you'll want**

Two cone wrenches in the right size (usually 13/15mm or 15/17mm), a spoke wrench that actually fits your nipples, a decent grease, and something to hold the wheel. A proper truing stand is nice but I did my first several wheels with zip ties on the chainstays as indicators and it worked fine.

**Stripping the hub**

Cassette off, then quick-release skewer out. Put a cone wrench on the flat of the cone on the non-drive side and a normal wrench on the locknut, and crack the locknut loose. Everything comes off that side — locknut, any spacers, the cone. Keep them in order on the bench. Photograph the stack if you think you might forget it, because you will.

Lift the axle out from the drive side and the bearings will come with it or drop into the cups. Count them. Most rear hubs run nine 1/4" balls per side, but count anyway — a rebuild one ball short will never adjust properly.

Wipe both cups clean with a rag and look at them under good light. What you want to see is a smooth polished track. What you're looking for is pitting: small dark craters spaced evenly around the race. Light pitting you can live with. Deep pitting means the hub shell is done and the wheel is a candidate for relacing onto a new hub, not a rebuild. Check the cones the same way, and replace them if they're marked — cones are cheap and consumable, the cups aren't.

**Reassembly and cone adjustment**

Pack the cups with grease and press the new balls in. The grease holds them while you thread the axle through. Don't add an extra ball because there's room; the correct count leaves a gap of about one ball's width.

Cone adjustment is the part people get wrong. Thread the cone down until it just contacts, back it off slightly, then hold the cone with the cone wrench and tighten the locknut against it. The act of locking will tighten the adjustment a little, so you want it a hair loose before you lock it.

Target: with the axle in your fingers, no detectable side-to-side play, and the axle spins freely under its own weight. Then remember that clamping the quick-release compresses the axle and takes up a bit more slack. So set it with a *barely* perceptible amount of play out of the frame — the kind you can just feel if you rock the rim — and it'll come out right once clamped. Install the wheel and check by rocking the rim laterally. Any knock, back it off and redo.

**Truing**

Lateral first. Spin the wheel, find where it kisses your indicator, and work on the worst spot. To pull the rim left, tighten left-side spokes and loosen right-side ones in that zone. Quarter turns, and spread the correction over three or four spokes rather than cranking one. Stress-relieve every few passes by grabbing pairs of parallel spokes and squeezing hard — you'll hear pinging, which is the spokes seating. If you skip this the wheel goes out of true on your first ride.

Radial (up-and-down hop) after lateral. Move the indicator to the outer edge of the rim. High spots get tightened on both sides equally; low spots get loosened on both sides. Radial corrections tend to disturb lateral true, so expect to go back and forth two or three times.

On tension: by feel means plucking each spoke and listening for a consistent pitch — drive-side rear spokes will ring noticeably higher than non-drive side, and that's normal, so compare like with like. Feel gets you to "consistent." It doesn't tell you whether consistent is also *correct*, which is where a tension meter earns its money. I built wheels by ear for years and they held up, but the first time I put a meter on one of them I found I'd been running about 15% under spec across the board. Borrow one if you can. If not, aim for even and slightly firmer than you think.

Ride it, then re-check true after fifty miles or so. Something always settles.
