Short version: your tank isn't "dirty" or "clean," it's a bacteria farm, and cycling means growing the farm before anything lives in it.

## What the nitrogen cycle actually is

Fish excrete ammonia (NH₃/NH₄⁺), and so does anything decaying in the tank — uneaten food, dead plant matter. Ammonia is acutely toxic; it damages gills at concentrations under 1 ppm.

Two groups of bacteria fix this, and they colonise your filter media and substrate, not the water:

1. **Ammonia oxidisers** convert ammonia → nitrite (NO₂⁻). Nitrite is also toxic — it binds to haemoglobin and stops oxygen transport.
2. **Nitrite oxidisers** convert nitrite → nitrate (NO₃⁻). Nitrate is comparatively harmless at the levels a home tank reaches, and you remove it with water changes or let plants take it up.

A "cycled" tank is one where both colonies are large enough to process your entire daily ammonia load to nitrate within roughly 24 hours. The important consequence: the colony sizes itself to the food supply. Bacteria don't care that you *intend* to add ten fish next week.

## Fishless cycling

You add ammonia yourself instead of making fish produce it. Nothing suffers, and you can push the ammonia higher than any fish would tolerate, which grows a bigger colony faster.

**What you need**

- Pure ammonia, no surfactants, no perfume. Shake the bottle; if it foams, don't use it. Ammonium chloride solutions sold for this purpose are more predictable.
- A liquid test kit for ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate. Strips are not accurate enough for nitrite at the range that matters.
- The filter running the whole time, heater at roughly 26–28 °C. Warmer is faster. Bacteria are aerobic, so good flow and surface agitation help.

**Procedure**

Dose ammonia to about **2 ppm**. Work out how much your bottle needs per litre once, write it on the bottle, and stop guessing thereafter. Higher isn't better — above about 5 ppm you start inhibiting the very bacteria you're growing.

Then test daily and record it. A typical run looks like this, though yours will differ:

- **Days 1–7:** ammonia sits at 2 ppm, nothing else moves. This stage is boring and everyone panics during it. Don't re-dose.
- **Days 7–14:** ammonia begins dropping, nitrite appears. Once ammonia reaches 0, re-dose back to 2 ppm. From here on, re-dose every time ammonia hits 0.
- **Days 14–30:** nitrite climbs, often to the top of the test scale, and stays there for what feels like forever. This is normal — nitrite oxidisers are slower growers. Nitrate starts appearing.
- **Days 25–45:** nitrite finally crashes to 0.

**The test that says you're done:** dose to 2 ppm ammonia in the evening. Test 24 hours later. If ammonia is 0 **and** nitrite is 0, you're cycled. One clean result is enough; two consecutive days is reassurance.

Then do a large water change — 50–80% — because nitrate will be very high by now, before adding fish. Add fish within a day or two of that final dose, and don't add all of them at once. The colony is sized for 2 ppm/day; a light initial stocking gives it room to adjust.

## Things that go wrong

- **Stalled at high nitrite.** Usually pH crashing. Nitrification consumes carbonate hardness; if KH hits zero, pH falls below ~6.5 and the bacteria slow dramatically. Test KH, and do a water change or add a little crushed coral.
- **Dosing dechlorinator obsessively.** Some conditioners temporarily bind ammonia and will confuse your readings.
- **Chlorinated top-ups.** Chlorine kills the colony. Treat every drop of tap water going in.
- **"Bottled bacteria" products.** Some genuinely help and can cut the timeline; many are dead on the shelf. Treat any speed-up as a bonus, not a plan.

Best accelerator by far is a handful of used filter media or a squeeze of mulm from an established, healthy tank. That can take a cycle from five weeks to one.
