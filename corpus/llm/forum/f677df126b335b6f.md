Following up on my own thread since a few of you asked how it went.

Short version: yes, it was worth it, and it took about four hours spread over two evenings. Longer version below for anyone doing this for the first time.

**Setup**

I used a granite surface plate, 12x18, the cheap import kind that runs about sixty bucks. You do not need a certified grade A plate for this. Any flat reference will do — plenty of people use a jointer bed or a piece of thick float glass. What you cannot use is the top of your table saw unless you have actually checked it, because a lot of them aren't as flat as their reputation suggests.

I stuck the paper down with 3M Super 77 sprayed on the paper, not on the granite. Spraying the stone means you spend the next hour scraping adhesive off it. Ask me how I know.

Grit progression: 80, 120, 220, 320, 400. I've seen people start at 60 on a really bad sole and I've seen people insist you should never go below 120. Mine had visible pitting near the mouth, so 80 it was.

**Important — keep the plane assembled**

Leave the frog, lever cap, and blade in place, with the blade retracted so it doesn't touch the paper. The cast iron body flexes under the tension of the lever cap, and if you flatten it disassembled you'll get a sole that's flat in a state it's never actually in while you're using it. This tripped me up on my first plane years ago.

**Checking progress**

Marker on the sole, then a few strokes, then look at what's left. That's the whole technique. The high spots lose their ink first. You're done at a given grit when the ink is gone everywhere along the toe, the mouth, and the heel.

Those three areas are the ones that matter. The sides of the sole and the area between the mouth and the heel can stay slightly low forever and it will not affect a single cut you make.

I also checked with a straightedge against a backlight every twenty minutes or so. Mine started with a noticeable hollow just behind the mouth and a high spot at the toe, which is a common wear pattern on a plane that's actually been used rather than left in a drawer.

**Time**

80 grit took by far the longest — probably two and a half hours of the four, and my forearms let me know about it the next day. Everything after 220 went quickly, maybe fifteen minutes per grit. Refresh the paper more often than feels necessary; worn abrasive just burnishes and wastes your time.

**Result**

Takes a full-width shaving now on quartersawn white oak without chatter. Total investment was the plane at $18, the surface plate, and a pack of wet/dry sheets. I'd do it again.
