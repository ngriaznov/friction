The short version: from a Bortle 8 backyard, a broadband filter will make your emission nebula images look slightly less brown, and a narrowband filter will make them possible. They are not two points on the same scale — they work by different mechanisms and they fail differently.

**What each one actually does**

A broadband light-pollution filter (UHC, CLS, "city light suppression," whatever the marketing name) is a notch-rejection filter. It has a couple of passbands 20–100 nm wide that happen to contain the astronomically interesting lines, and it blocks the gaps in between — historically the mercury and sodium emission lines from streetlights. A typical UHC passes roughly 480–520 nm (covering Hβ and OIII) and a second window around 650–660 nm (Hα), and rejects most of what's between.

A narrowband filter passes one line at a time through a window 3–12 nm wide. Everything else, including nearly all of the sky background, is simply gone.

**Why this matters more now than it used to**

Your competitor isn't the light, it's the *shot noise* on the light. Sky glow contributes signal you subtract off, but the Poisson noise on that signal you cannot subtract — it stays in the image forever, and it scales as the square root of the background flux. Cutting background flux by 10× cuts your background noise by about 3.2×.

The catch is that the entire notch-filter concept was built for a sky lit by discrete emission lines. LED streetlights are broadband continuum across the whole visible range. There is no longer a convenient gap for a broadband filter to sit in. In an LED-dominated sky, a UHC might buy you 1–1.5 magnitudes of background suppression, sometimes less. A 3 nm narrowband filter is still throwing away 99%+ of that continuum, because the physics doesn't care what shape the source spectrum is — it only passes 3 nm.

So: broadband LP filters have gotten meaningfully worse over the last fifteen years. Narrowband has not.

**Where a UHC still earns its place**

- **Visual observing.** Genuinely excellent, and this is what most UHC filters were designed for.
- **Reflection nebulae, galaxies, star clusters** — anything with a continuum spectrum. A narrowband filter is useless here; you'd be filtering out the target.
- **Fast optics.** Interference filters shift their passband blueward with increasing angle of incidence. At f/2 a 3 nm filter can shift enough that Hα walks off the edge of the passband, causing vignetting and lost signal. Below about f/4, either use a wider narrowband (7–12 nm) or a filter specifically specced for fast systems.
- **You're on a budget and shoot mixed targets.** £90 versus £300.

**When the dual narrowband is worth it**

If you shoot emission nebulae from a city with a one-shot-colour camera, buy the dual narrowband. This is the least hedged advice in this answer.

A dual narrowband passes an Hα window and an OIII window simultaneously. On an OSC sensor the Bayer matrix separates them for you — Hα lands in the red pixels, OIII in the green and blue — so you get two-channel narrowband data in a single exposure run. That's the trick that makes OSC narrowband practical at all. The difference between a UHC and a 3–7 nm dual band on something like the North America Nebula from Bortle 8 is not subtle: it's the difference between fighting gradients through six hours of integration and having usable signal in ninety minutes.

If you shoot mono with a filter wheel, buy individual 3–7 nm filters instead; a dual band on mono wastes the advantage.

Practical order of purchase for an OSC imager in a bright city: dual narrowband first, better mount second, everything else after that.
