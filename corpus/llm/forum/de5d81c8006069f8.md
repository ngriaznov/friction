Your starter isn't dead — it's hungry, and the dark liquid is the evidence.

That layer is called hooch. It's a weak alcohol solution the yeast produce once they've eaten through the available starch and shifted from vigorous aerobic growth to just surviving. It shows up when a starter has gone too long between feedings, and the darker it is, the longer it's been sitting. Grey is a day or two past due. Dark brown or nearly black means considerably longer.

The lack of rise fits the same story. A two-week-old starter has a real yeast population, but at this point the culture is also heavily acidic — all those hours of fermentation with nothing left to eat drive the pH down far enough that the yeast themselves become sluggish. You've effectively pickled them. They're alive, they're just not in a state where they can produce gas fast enough to lift the flour.

**The fix**

Pour off the hooch. Don't stir it back in — some people do, and it works, but it adds more acid and alcohol to a culture that already has too much of both. Right now you want to dilute, not concentrate.

Then feed more often and with a heavier dilution than you probably have been. Discard down to about 20 g of starter, then add 40 g of flour and 40 g of water. That's a 1:2:2 ratio by weight, and it's aggressive on purpose: the small amount of old starter carries less acid forward into the new mixture, giving the yeast a friendlier pH to work in.

Do that twice a day, roughly twelve hours apart, at room temperature. Somewhere warm helps — high 70s Fahrenheit is comfortable; a cold kitchen will slow everything down and make you think the fix isn't working.

**On hydration**

If you've been feeding at a very high hydration — a loose, batter-like starter — thicken it up to equal weights of flour and water. A stiffer starter ferments more slowly and holds structure better, so you'll actually see the rise instead of having it collapse before you check.

Adding a spoonful of whole rye or whole wheat to each feeding also helps a lot. There are more minerals and more wild yeast on the bran, and stalled starters often perk up noticeably after two or three rye feedings.

**What to expect**

Give it three days. By the second or third feeding cycle you should see it roughly double within four to six hours of eating, and the smell should move from sharp and solventy toward something closer to yogurt. Once it's reliably doubling on schedule, it's ready to bake with.

If after a week of consistent twice-daily feeding there's no rise at all and no bubbling, that's a different problem — but hooch plus a stall after two weeks is the most ordinary thing a starter does, and it almost always comes back.
