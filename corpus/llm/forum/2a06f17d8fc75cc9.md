This is general information about how rebalancing works mechanically, not a recommendation about what any particular person should do. Anything involving your own tax situation is worth running past a professional.

---

**What rebalancing actually is**

A three-fund portfolio is defined by target weights — say 60% total US stock, 20% total international stock, 20% total bond. Markets move those weights around. After a strong equity year the same portfolio might sit at 68/22/10. It is now a different portfolio than the one you designed, with meaningfully more risk than you signed up for.

Rebalancing is the act of trading back toward the targets. Mechanically:

1. Value every holding and the total portfolio.
2. Compute each holding's current weight (`holding value ÷ total`).
3. Compute each target dollar amount (`target weight × total`).
4. The difference is what you buy or sell.

Worked example. Portfolio worth $500,000 sitting at 68/22/10:

| Fund | Current | Current $ | Target $ | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US stock | 68% | $340,000 | $300,000 | Sell $40,000 |
| Intl stock | 22% | $110,000 | $100,000 | Sell $10,000 |
| Bonds | 10% | $50,000 | $100,000 | Buy $50,000 |

That's the whole calculation. Everything else is a question of *when* you run it.

**Calendar rebalancing**

You pick an interval — annually, semiannually, quarterly — and rebalance on that date regardless of how far off the weights have drifted. If it's January 15th and you're at 61/20/19, you trade the small amounts anyway.

Pros: trivially simple, no monitoring, and the decision is made in advance so you don't have to exercise judgment during a scary market. Cons: you can trade when there's nothing meaningful to fix, and you can sit at a badly drifted allocation for eleven months waiting for the date.

**Threshold-band rebalancing**

You set a tolerance around each target and only act when a holding breaches it. Bands come in two flavors:

- *Absolute* bands: rebalance when a holding is more than 5 percentage points from target. A 60% target triggers below 55% or above 65%.
- *Relative* bands: rebalance when a holding is more than 25% of its own weight away from target. A 20% target triggers below 15% or above 25%; a 60% target triggers below 45% or above 75%. This scales the tolerance to position size, so small holdings get tighter bands in absolute terms.

Pros: you trade when drift actually matters and ignore noise. Cons: it requires checking, and in a fast-moving market it can trigger at inconvenient moments.

The two are often combined: check on a fixed schedule, but only trade if a band is breached. That caps monitoring effort while keeping trades tied to real drift.

**Tax treatment differs sharply by account type**

Inside a tax-advantaged retirement account (401(k), IRA, and similar), selling generates no taxable event. You can rebalance as precisely and as often as you like; the only friction is trading costs, which for major index funds are usually zero.

Inside a taxable brokerage account, selling an appreciated position realizes a capital gain. Held over a year, it's taxed at long-term rates; under a year, at ordinary income rates. Selling $40,000 of stock that has $15,000 of embedded gain creates a real tax bill this year, which is a genuine cost that the calendar-versus-band choice can't make disappear.

Common mechanics people use to reduce taxable selling:

- **Rebalance with new contributions.** Direct all new money into the underweight fund. If contributions are large relative to portfolio size, this can hold the allocation in range without selling anything.
- **Rebalance with dividends.** Turn off automatic reinvestment and direct distributions to whatever is underweight.
- **Do the selling in the retirement account.** If you hold all three funds in both account types, you can shift weights by trading only inside the sheltered account and leave the taxable holdings untouched.
- **Pair with loss harvesting.** Realized losses offset realized gains, so a rebalance done alongside harvesting can net out to a smaller tax bill.

A last mechanical note: watch the wash-sale rule if you're selling at a loss and buying something similar within 30 days, and watch whether a taxable sale pushes you across a bracket boundary for long-term gains.
