P0420 does not mean "replace the catalytic converter." It means the ECU compared the upstream and downstream oxygen sensor signals and concluded the cat isn't storing enough oxygen. That conclusion is only as good as the two sensors feeding it, and on a Civic the downstream sensor is a far more common culprit than the cat. Check the inputs before you spend money on the output.

**Understand what the code actually measures**

The upstream (air/fuel ratio) sensor sits before the cat and swings constantly as the ECU trims fueling. The downstream (heated O2) sensor sits after the cat and should be relatively flat — a healthy cat buffers oxygen, so the downstream signal hovers around 0.6–0.75 V with only lazy movement. When the downstream signal starts mirroring the upstream one, the ECU sets P0420.

So there are three ways to get this code: the cat is genuinely worn, the downstream sensor is lying, or something upstream (exhaust leak, misfire, fuel trim problem) is destroying the cat's ability to work.

**Do this before touching the cat**

1. **Pull all codes, not just the stored one.** If you also have misfire codes, lean/rich codes, or an EVAP purge fault, fix those first. A cylinder that misfires dumps raw fuel into the cat and will kill a new one within months.

2. **Check fuel trims at idle and at 2500 RPM.** Long-term fuel trim beyond roughly ±10% points at a vacuum leak, a dirty MAF, or failing injectors. Fix the trims, then re-test.

3. **Watch live sensor data.** With a scan tool that shows both sensors:
   - Get the car to full operating temperature and drive it at steady cruise.
   - Upstream should oscillate; downstream should be a slow lazy line near 0.7 V.
   - Now snap the throttle. Downstream should lag noticeably behind upstream. If downstream tracks upstream nearly in step, either the cat is spent or the downstream sensor has failed.

4. **Distinguish a lazy sensor from a dead cat.** Force a rich condition (propane into the intake, or a brief snap-throttle) and watch downstream response time. A sluggish sensor takes over a second to react and often never reaches 0.9 V. A good sensor responds quickly but the *cat* is what keeps the line flat afterward.

5. **Look for exhaust leaks ahead of and around the downstream sensor.** A pinhole at the flex pipe or a leaking flange gasket pulls fresh air past the sensor and produces exactly this code with a perfectly good cat. On Civics of a certain age, check the flex section right after the manifold — it cracks.

6. **Temperature check.** With an IR thermometer, the outlet of a working cat should read meaningfully hotter than the inlet, typically 50–100°F. Roughly equal temps suggest it isn't lighting off.

**If all of that is clean**

Then the cat is a reasonable diagnosis. Note that on many Civics the primary cat is integrated into the exhaust manifold, so "replace the cat" is a bigger job than people expect. Buy OEM or a quality aftermarket unit — cheap universal cats commonly re-trigger P0420 within a few thousand miles because their substrate loading is too low to satisfy Honda's monitor.
