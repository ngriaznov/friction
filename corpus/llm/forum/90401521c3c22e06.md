Finally pulled the trigger on the NAS build I've been planning for about six months, wanted to write up the reasoning while it's still fresh in case it helps anyone else agonizing over the same vdev decision I was.

Went with a single RAIDZ2 vdev across 8 drives rather than 4 mirrored pairs (striped mirrors), and it came down to a fairly simple tradeoff once I actually sat down and did the math instead of just going with whatever the loudest opinion in whatever thread I was reading said. Mirrored vdevs give you better random IOPS since every mirror pair can service reads independently and resilver times are fast (just copying one drive to its new partner), and that matters a lot if you're running VMs or databases off the pool. But my workload is almost entirely large sequential media files and backups — Plex library, backup targets from a couple other machines — where IOPS barely matters and usable capacity does. With 8x 16TB drives, RAIDZ2 gets me 6 drives worth of usable space (96TB raw parity capacity) versus 4 drives worth in a striped-mirror layout of the same 8 disks (would only get 64TB usable, since half of every pair is a copy). That's a huge capacity difference for my use case, and RAIDZ2's two-drive parity tolerance was the other deciding factor — I wanted to survive a second drive failure during a resilver window without holding my breath, especially at 16TB per drive where resilvers can run the better part of a day even on decent hardware.

I know the standard objection to wide RAIDZ vdevs is resilver time and the stress that puts on the remaining drives, and that's fair, but 8 drives isn't really "wide" in the sense people mean when they say RAIDZ doesn't scale — that concern is much more relevant at 12+ drives in a single vdev. At 8 wide with Z2 I was comfortable with the risk profile, especially since these are drives from two different purchase batches specifically to avoid correlated failure from a bad batch or shared manufacturing defect.

On ashift: this is the one thing I'd tell anyone building a pool to slow down and get right before creating it, because you cannot change it after the fact without destroying and rebuilding the pool. Ashift sets the minimum block size ZFS uses on the underlying devices, expressed as a power of 2 — ashift=12 means 4K (2^12) sectors, ashift=9 means 512-byte sectors. My drives (Seagate Exos, in this case) report a 512-byte logical sector size but a 4Kn... actually a 4K physical sector size under the hood (512e emulation), which is extremely common on modern spinning drives regardless of what they report to the OS. If you let ZFS auto-detect based on the logical sector size it can pick ashift=9 on drives that are physically 4K, and every write smaller than 4K then becomes a read-modify-write at the physical level instead of a clean aligned write — that's a real, sustained performance hit, not a theoretical one. I checked the actual physical sector size with smartctl (`smartctl -a /dev/sdX` and look at the physical vs logical sector size lines) rather than trusting auto-detection, and forced ashift=12 at pool creation with `zpool create -o ashift=12`.

What I'd do differently with hindsight: I'd have bought the drives from three sources instead of two, purely for batch diversity, and I underestimated how much I'd want hot-swap bays after the fact — went with a case that has them but only for 8 of the 10 total bays, and I'm already regretting not having spare bays for an eventual pool expansion via a second vdev down the line, since you can't just add drives to an existing RAIDZ vdev to grow it (well, you technically can now with newer OpenZFS raidz expansion, but I hadn't accounted for needing physical space for a second vdev entirely). Also would have run memtest for way longer than I did before trusting the pool with real data — did about 4 hours, should have done 24, since ZFS is famously unforgiving of bad RAM corrupting data on the way in.

Happy to answer questions if anyone's mid-decision on their own build.
