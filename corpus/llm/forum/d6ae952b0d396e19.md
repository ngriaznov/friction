The short version: start with `Type=simple`, and move to `Type=notify` once your daemon can tell systemd when it is genuinely ready. The watchdog is only useful with the latter.

**Type=simple vs Type=notify**

With `Type=simple`, systemd considers the unit started the instant it has forked and exec'd your binary. That is before your daemon has parsed its config, bound its socket, or connected to anything. Any unit with `After=yourdaemon.service` will start immediately, possibly against a service that is not listening yet. For a lot of daemons this is fine; for anything with dependents it is a race you will eventually lose.

With `Type=notify`, systemd holds the unit in "activating" until your process sends `READY=1` over the `$NOTIFY_SOCKET` datagram socket. From C that is `sd_notify(0, "READY=1")` after your setup completes. Other languages have equivalents, or you can write the datagram yourself — it is a few lines against an `AF_UNIX` socket whose path systemd puts in the environment. `systemctl start` blocks until you send it, so a daemon that never sends `READY=1` will hang until `TimeoutStartSec` expires.

**The watchdog**

`WatchdogSec=` sets a deadline. Once you have sent `READY=1`, your process must send `WATCHDOG=1` at least that often or systemd treats the service as hung and applies `WatchdogSignal` (SIGABRT by default), then restarts per your `Restart=` policy.

Two things people get wrong:

1. Ping at roughly half the interval, not at the interval. systemd exports the value in microseconds as `$WATCHDOG_USEC`; read it rather than hardcoding, so changing the unit file does not silently break the timing.
2. Send the keepalive from a place that is actually blocked when your daemon is stuck. A dedicated timer thread that pings on schedule will happily keep pinging while your main loop is deadlocked, which defeats the entire mechanism. Ping from the main event loop.

**Restart policy**

`Restart=on-failure` restarts on a nonzero exit, an uncaught signal, or a watchdog timeout, but *not* on a clean exit(0) — which is usually what you want, since it lets the daemon shut itself down deliberately. Pair it with the rate limiter, or a daemon that crashes instantly on a bad config will spin forever:

**Example unit**

```ini
[Unit]
Description=Example daemon
After=network-online.target
Wants=network-online.target

[Service]
Type=notify
ExecStart=/usr/local/bin/exampled --config /etc/exampled.conf
NotifyAccess=main
WatchdogSec=30s
Restart=on-failure
RestartSec=5s
StartLimitIntervalSec=300
StartLimitBurst=5

User=exampled
Group=exampled
ProtectSystem=strict
ProtectHome=yes
PrivateTmp=yes
NoNewPrivileges=yes
RuntimeDirectory=exampled

[Install]
WantedBy=multi-user.target
```

`NotifyAccess=main` restricts notifications to the main process; the default is `main` for `Type=notify` anyway, but being explicit avoids surprises if you later fork helpers.

Note that `StartLimitIntervalSec`/`StartLimitBurst` belong in `[Unit]` on systemd older than v229. On current versions they are accepted in `[Service]`. If your unit needs to run on both, put them in `[Unit]`.

Finally: do not daemonize. No forking into the background, no PID file, no `setsid`. Log to stdout/stderr and let journald collect it. `Type=forking` exists for legacy daemons and brings PID-tracking problems you do not need to inherit.
