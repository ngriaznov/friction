Promised a few people in the "hive from hell" thread that I'd post a proper account once this was settled. It's settled. Long post, sorry.

**Background**

Hive 3, second-year colony, superseded on its own last summer. Fine through autumn. By late May this year it had turned genuinely nasty — bees meeting me at ten metres, following me the length of the garden, and stinging the gloves before I'd even cracked the crownboard. My neighbour got hit twice while mowing. That's the line for me. It wasn't a bad-day thing, it was every inspection for six weeks running, in all weather.

Ordered a mated Buckfast queen from a breeder about an hour away. Picked her up rather than posted — I'd rather drive than have her sit in a depot.

**Finding the old queen**

This was the hard part and I want to be honest about how badly it went the first time.

First attempt, a Tuesday evening: went in, worked slowly, got maybe six frames deep and the colony boiled. I couldn't see anything through the mass of bees on the frames and I was getting hit hard enough that I closed up and walked away. Wasted trip, angry bees, angry me.

Second attempt three days later, done properly:

- Went in at 11am on a warm, still day so the maximum number of foragers were out.
- Moved the whole hive about three metres to one side and put an empty box on the original stand. Flying bees returned to the empty box instead of joining the fight. That single step made more difference than anything else.
- Smoked lightly. I'd been over-smoking previously and I think it was making them worse.
- Took each frame out and immediately shook the bees into the empty box after checking it, working through the brood nest frame by frame so the population in the box I was searching kept dropping.

Found her on frame seven, unmarked, on a face I'd already glanced at once. Small, dark, moving fast. Pinched her, which I dislike doing but there it is.

**Timing before introduction**

Here's where I did something a bit different from the standard advice. Conventional wisdom is to leave 24 hours queenless before introducing so they're properly aware and receptive.

I went longer — I waited a full six days, then went through and destroyed every emergency cell they'd drawn. There were eleven of them. My reasoning: with a colony this defensive I wanted absolutely no chance of them raising a daughter of the old queen, because the aggression is genetic and I'd be back here in eight weeks. Knocking cells at day six means anything they started is visible and none of it has emerged.

Immediately after the cell knockdown I put the new queen in, still in her cage, candy plug intact, tape over the candy end. Cage wedged between two brood frames, screen facing outward so they could contact her.

Left it 48 hours. Went back, checked the cage without pulling frames — bees on it were calm, not gripping the mesh or biting at it — and pulled the tape off the candy. Closed up.

**Confirming acceptance**

Left them completely alone for seven days. Hardest part of the whole thing.

Day 7 after release: cage empty and discarded on the floor, no queen cells, colony noticeably quieter to work. Didn't spot her but wasn't going to keep digging.

Day 11: found eggs. Single eggs, centred in the cells, in a tight pattern across two frames. That was the confirmation I wanted — laying worker or a failed introduction would have looked nothing like that. Spotted her on the third frame I lifted, marked green, walking calmly.

**Where things are now**

Six weeks on and the difference in temperament is difficult to overstate. I inspected last Sunday in a veil and no gloves. Nobody followed me back to the house.

The genetics really were the whole problem. If you're on the fence about requeening a hot colony because it feels drastic — it isn't. It's the fix.
