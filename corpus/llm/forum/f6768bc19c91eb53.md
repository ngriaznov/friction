**Q: How do I diagnose BGP route flapping between two upstream ISPs on a multihomed router?**

Route flapping — a prefix repeatedly withdrawn and re-announced — is one of those problems that's easy to notice and annoying to pin down, because the symptom (intermittent reachability, jittery latency) shows up long after the actual instability on the wire. Here's how I'd approach it on a router multihomed to two upstreams.

**Start with flap statistics, not the RIB**

Your first stop should be per-neighbor flap counters, not the routing table itself. On most platforms:

```
show bgp neighbors <peer-ip> | include (flap|last error)
show bgp ipv4 unicast flap-statistics
```

You're looking for a specific prefix (or set of prefixes) with a high flap count concentrated on one peering session rather than spread evenly. If flaps are evenly distributed across both upstreams, the instability is probably in your own local network, not upstream. If it's concentrated on one peer, that's your first real lead.

Also check `show bgp neighbors <peer-ip>` for the "Connections established" counter and the uptime. A session that's reset five times in the last hour tells a different story than a session that's been up for a week but keeps withdrawing and re-announcing individual prefixes without the session itself dropping — the former points at a physical or session-layer problem (interface flap, MTU mismatch, keepalive timeout), the latter points at something upstream-side, like the peer's own route churn being propagated to you.

**Correlate with interface and link stats**

Before blaming BGP, check the physical layer to that peer:

```
show interface <interface> | include (error|drop|CRC)
show interface <interface> counters errors
```

A flapping physical link or a marginal fiber connection will produce exactly this symptom — the session bounces, withdraws everything it learned, then re-establishes and re-advertises. If you see CRC errors or input drops climbing in step with your flap timestamps, you've found it, and it's not really a BGP problem at all.

**Dampening: helps sometimes, hides the real issue other times**

Route flap dampening (`bgp dampening`) suppresses a prefix from being re-advertised after it exceeds a flap penalty threshold, decaying that penalty over time. It's useful when you're on the receiving end of flaps from someone else's instability and want to protect your own control plane and downstream peers from constant churn — a flapping /24 three hops away shouldn't be reprocessed by your RIB fifty times an hour.

The trap is using dampening as a substitute for actually fixing the flap. If the flapping prefix is *your own* announcement, dampening it doesn't stop the underlying instability — it just delays how quickly the rest of the internet finds out about it, and during the suppression window your prefix is effectively unreachable from networks honoring the dampening, which is often worse than the flap itself. Dampening belongs on ingress filtering for prefixes you're *receiving*, applied conservatively (RFC 7196 argued fairly convincingly that aggressive dampening does more harm than good on the wider internet, and a lot of operators dialed it back or removed it entirely after that).

**Tracing back to a specific peer link**

If you're multihomed and only one upstream shows the flap, you're most of the way there — check that specific physical circuit, the router interface facing it, and if it's a private peering, ask that ISP's NOC whether they see flaps on their side of the session too. If they don't see anything and you do, look hard at your own interface — mismatched duplex settings, a bad SFP, or a shared line card issue affecting just that port are common culprits.

If both sides look clean but the prefix itself still flaps, check whether the instability originates further upstream and is simply being relayed to you — `show bgp <prefix> history` or an looking glass at a few hops out can show whether the AS path itself is stable while only the reachability toggles, which usually means someone else's flap is propagating through your upstream and there isn't much to fix locally beyond dampening it on ingress.
