So I spent most of yesterday afternoon doing something I'd been putting off for two weeks: cleaning up the commit history on a feature branch before sending it out for review. 40-ish commits, and I mean that in the worst way - "wip", "fix", "actually fix", "why doesn't this work", a couple of "temp debug please ignore" commits that definitely should not have made it into version control. Figured I'd write up how it went since I know a few people here have asked about interactive rebase before and were nervous about it.

Started with `git rebase -i main` (branched off main, so that gave me the full list of commits unique to my branch). The editor pops up with all 40 commits listed `pick`, oldest first, which is honestly the most useful part of the whole exercise just as a review tool - seeing the shape of what I actually did laid out like that made it obvious which commits were real logical units and which were just me flailing.

For the flailing ones I used `fixup` (shortened to `f`) rather than `squash` - fixup folds the commit in without asking you to edit the combined message, squash stops and lets you merge/edit both messages. Since my "wip" and "fix typo" commits didn't have anything worth keeping in their messages, fixup was the right call almost every time. Ended up combining maybe 25 of those junk commits down into about 6 real ones this way, just changing `pick` to `f` on the ones that belonged folded into the commit above them and reordering a couple of lines where a fix had landed several commits after the thing it was fixing.

Hit a conflict about halfway through, unsurprisingly - reordering commits means git has to replay changes in a different sequence than they originally happened, so if two commits touch overlapping lines the replay can conflict even though the original sequential application never did. Rebase paused with `CONFLICT (content): Merge conflict in src/handlers/auth.js`, dropped me into the working tree with conflict markers. Nothing too scary - resolved it by hand, `git add` the file, then `git rebase --continue` and it picked back up from where it stopped. The one thing I'd tell anyone doing this for the first time: don't panic and don't run `--abort` at the first sign of a conflict, it's usually a two minute fix and aborting just means doing all the work you'd already done a second time.

After the fixups landed I went back through with `reword` on the remaining commits to write actual messages - what changed and why, not "update auth.js". Ended up with 6 clean commits instead of 40 messy ones, force-pushed to my branch (`git push --force-with-lease`, not plain `--force`, since someone else could theoretically have pulled from it), and opened the PR. Reviewer commented that it was the easiest diff they'd looked at all month, which felt good after an afternoon of it.

Worth the time. Would not want to do it on a branch shared with other active contributors though - that's a different, much more careful conversation.
