# I finally learned to read a flame graph

For years I treated flame graphs the way I treat a chest X-ray: obviously meaningful to someone, opaque to me. I'd generate one, squint, decide the widest orange thing was probably bad, and go back to adding timers by hand. What changed my mind was `/v2/reports/summary`, an endpoint on our reporting service that took 4.1 seconds at p95 and had resisted two separate attempts to speed it up.

## The one rule that unlocked it

Here is the thing nobody told me plainly: **the horizontal axis is not time.** It's not a timeline. Frames aren't ordered left to right by when they ran — they're ordered alphabetically, which is to say arbitrarily.

Width is the only thing that means anything horizontally, and what it means is: what fraction of collected samples had this function somewhere on the stack. Height is just call depth. A tall narrow spike is a deep call chain that barely runs. A short wide plateau is something cheap that runs constantly.

Once I stopped trying to read it left-to-right like a waterfall, everything got easier.

## What the report endpoint actually looked like

I profiled the endpoint under a replayed production load and got a graph where the top-level handler frame was, obviously, 100% wide. One level down it split into three: request parsing (thin), `build_summary` (about 88% of the width), and serialization (the rest).

So far, so unsurprising. The interesting part is what happened inside `build_summary`. I expected one fat child. Instead I got a *plateau* — a wide, flat band of the same function repeated: `Account.load_settings`, taking roughly 60% of total width, sitting at nearly the same depth across a huge stretch of the graph.

That shape is the signature of a loop calling something expensive. Not one slow call — hundreds of fast-ish ones. That's why my hand-placed timers had never caught it: each individual call was under two milliseconds and looked completely reasonable in a log line.

Underneath `load_settings` the graph went one level deeper into the ORM's `execute`, and under that, `socket.recv`. Blocking on the database, once per account, inside a loop over about 400 accounts. A textbook N+1, hiding in plain sight behind an innocuous accessor that lazily fetched settings the first time you touched them.

## The fix, and what the graph looked like after

The change was six lines: preload settings for every account in the report scope with one query, hand the dict into `build_summary`, and drop the lazy accessor's fallback path behind a flag so we'd find out if anything else depended on it.

p95 went from 4.1s to 480ms.

The new flame graph was genuinely satisfying to look at. The `load_settings` plateau collapsed into a single narrow tower — one query, still visibly there, maybe 8% of width. And with that plateau gone, the graph's proportions changed completely: serialization, which had been an unremarkable sliver, was suddenly the widest thing on the page at around 35%. It hadn't gotten slower. It was just no longer being drowned out.

That's the second lesson. A flame graph shows you *relative* cost, always. Fix the biggest thing and the graph redraws itself around the next biggest thing, which may be something you'd never have thought to look at.

## What I'd tell past me

Look for plateaus, not peaks. Ignore the horizontal order entirely. Check what's at the *bottom* of the widest towers — that's where the actual work (or the actual waiting) happens. And re-profile after every fix, because the picture you get is only ever true relative to the current worst offender.
