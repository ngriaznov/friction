# Forty minutes to eight

Our CI pipeline took forty minutes to tell us whether a pull request was safe to merge. Nobody waited for it. People opened a PR, went to lunch, came back, and found a red build caused by something they'd forgotten they wrote. The feedback loop was so long it had stopped being feedback.

We spent about three weeks chipping away at it, on and off. It's down to eight minutes now. Here's where the time actually went, which was not where I assumed it would.

## Measuring first, because my guesses were wrong

Before touching anything, I added timing output to every stage and let it run for a week. My assumption going in was that the unit tests were the problem — there are about 4,200 of them and they felt slow. They weren't. They took six minutes. The breakdown looked like this:

- dependency install: 9 minutes
- unit tests: 6 minutes
- end-to-end tests: 22 minutes
- lint, typecheck, build: 3 minutes

So the two things I'd never thought about were two thirds of the runtime.

## The install step was pure waste

Nine minutes, every single run, to download the same packages we downloaded on the previous run. We had a cache configured. It had never worked, and nobody had checked, because a cache miss looks exactly like a cache hit from the outside — the build just goes.

The key was hashed over the whole repo tree instead of the lockfile. Every commit invalidated it. Fixing the key to hash only `package-lock.json` took one line and dropped installs from nine minutes to about forty seconds on a hit. That was the single highest-value change of the whole effort, and it was a typo-level bug that had been sitting there for a year and a half.

Worth saying out loud: if you have a cache in CI, go and verify it hits. Print the hit/miss. Ours had been silently missing since the day it was added.

## Cutting E2E tests that tested nothing new

The end-to-end suite had 180 browser tests. Reading through them, the pattern was obvious: whenever someone touched checkout, they added a full E2E test for their case. We had eleven tests that all walked through login, add-to-cart, checkout, and confirmation, differing only in which payment method they picked or which discount code they applied.

Each one cost roughly ninety seconds of real browser time to re-verify the same eight steps.

We kept one full-journey test per critical flow — checkout, signup, password reset, subscription upgrade — and pushed the variations down to integration tests hitting the API directly, where the same assertions run in under a second. That took the E2E suite from 180 tests to 41, and from 22 minutes to 7.

The nervous part was deleting tests. What helped was framing it as moving coverage rather than removing it: for every E2E test we cut, we pointed at the API-level test that covered the same branch. We wrote that mapping down in the PR description. Three months on, we haven't had a checkout bug slip through, though I'll admit that's a small sample.

## Parallelization, last

Only after the above did splitting across runners make sense — there's no point paying for four machines to run a nine-minute cache miss four times.

We shard the unit tests across four runners by file, balanced by historical duration rather than alphabetically. Alphabetical splitting gave us one shard at 4 minutes and one at 40 seconds, which means your suite is as slow as its worst shard. Recording per-file timings and greedily packing them evened the shards out to about 90 seconds each.

E2E runs on two runners, and that's mostly limited by how many browser instances our test account can handle without tripping rate limits.

## Where it landed

Eight minutes, wall clock, from push to green. Most of that is now the E2E suite, which is the honest floor for tests that drive a real browser.

The thing I keep thinking about is that two of the three wins were not clever. One was a broken cache key. One was deleting redundant tests nobody had ever reviewed as a group. The parallelization — the part that sounded like real engineering — was worth about four minutes.

Measure before you optimize. I know. But apparently I needed to relearn it.
