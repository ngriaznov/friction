# We cut our AWS bill from $18k to $9.4k without deleting anything

Our monthly AWS bill crossed $18,000 in February and our CFO asked, politely, whether that was expected. It was not expected. It was just the number nobody had looked at in fourteen months.

Four of us spent about three weeks on it, part-time. March came in at $9,400. We did not turn off a single service, migrate anything, or rewrite any code. Here's what actually moved the needle.

## Step one: find out where the money is

Cost Explorer grouped by service told us EC2 was 71% of the bill. That was the whole picture we had, because nothing was tagged.

So the first real work was tagging. We wrote a script that walked every running instance, matched its security groups and AMI against our Terraform state, and guessed an owning team. It got about 80% right; we fixed the rest by hand over two afternoons. Then we turned on cost allocation tags and waited 24 hours for the data to start flowing.

That wait was worth it. Once we could group by team and by workload, the shape of the problem was obvious: our batch processing fleet was 44% of EC2 spend, and our web tier was massively oversized.

## Rightsizing: boring, and the biggest win

The web tier ran on `m5.4xlarge` instances. Sixteen vCPUs, 64 GB of RAM. Twelve of them behind an ALB.

Peak CPU across the fleet, over 90 days: 11%. Peak memory: 9 GB.

Nobody chose that instance size for a reason. It was copied from a runbook someone wrote in 2021 for a service that no longer exists, and it propagated through three Terraform modules by copy-paste.

We moved the web tier to `m6i.xlarge` — four vCPUs, 16 GB — and went from twelve instances to fourteen for a bit more headroom during deploys. That alone took the web tier from roughly $6,100/month to $1,900. The newer generation was part of it; `m6i` is meaningfully cheaper per vCPU than `m5` and a little faster.

The one thing I'd warn about: check your network baseline before downsizing. We nearly moved a log shipper to a `t3.medium` and would have hit the burst credit wall within a day. Small instances have small network allowances, and that's not visible in CPU graphs.

We also found 31 unattached EBS volumes and nine idle NAT gateways in dev accounts. About $700/month, sitting there for over a year.

## Spot instances for batch

Our batch fleet ran nightly ETL, video transcoding, and a set of report generators. All of it was on-demand `c5.2xlarge`, running roughly six hours a night, plus a long tail of jobs during the day.

Every one of those workloads is interruptible. They're all idempotent, they all checkpoint, and if a job dies it gets re-queued. We had just never bothered.

Moving to spot took about a week of real work — most of it on handling the two-minute interruption notice properly. Our transcoder was the tricky one: it wrote output directly to the target S3 key, so an interruption mid-write left a truncated file. We changed it to write to a temp key and copy on completion.

We also stopped requesting one instance type. Spot pricing punishes you for being picky. We now request a pool of six instance families with similar specs and let the allocation strategy pick whatever's cheapest with the lowest interruption rate. Our average discount off on-demand is around 68%.

Batch went from about $5,400/month to $1,750. Interruption rate in practice: three or four jobs a week get killed and re-run. Nobody notices.

## The numbers

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Web tier | ~$6,100 | ~$1,900 |
| Batch | ~$5,400 | ~$1,750 |
| Waste (idle EBS, NAT) | ~$700 | ~$50 |
| Everything else | ~$5,900 | ~$5,700 |
| **Total** | **~$18,100** | **~$9,400** |

The "everything else" row is honest: RDS, S3, data transfer, and a Kafka cluster we didn't touch. That's the next project.

## What I'd tell you to do first

Tag things. Everything else follows from being able to see the bill by team and workload. We spent two days on tagging and it made the next three weeks possible.

And set a budget alert. We now get a Slack message when the month-to-date projection exceeds last month by more than 15%. It's fired twice, both times legitimately, and both times we caught it on day three instead of day thirty.
