Our AWS bill was sitting around $14,200 a month at the start of this year, and after a focused six-week push it's now running closer to $7,100. No architectural rewrite, no moving off AWS, nothing exotic — just rightsizing EC2 instances and moving batch workloads onto spot. Writing down the actual numbers because most cost-cutting posts I read are frustratingly vague about what "roughly half" means in real dollars.

The first thing we did was pull three months of CloudWatch CPU and memory utilization data across every instance we ran, sorted by size, and looked at what was actually being used against what was provisioned. It was worse than I expected. We had four `m5.2xlarge` instances (8 vCPU, 32GB RAM) running application servers that peaked at maybe 20% CPU and 35% memory under our heaviest traffic of the month, sized that way because someone had provisioned for a traffic spike eighteen months earlier that never recurred at that scale again. We stepped those down to `m5.xlarge`, and when even that showed comfortable headroom over the following two weeks, down again to `m5.large` for two of the four, keeping the other two at `m5.xlarge` as a buffer for actual peak periods. That single change took those four instances from a combined $1,120/month to about $410/month.

We found a similar story on our database read replicas — two `r5.xlarge` instances that existed mostly for redundancy rather than load, sized when we genuinely needed the headroom during an earlier growth spurt. We kept one at that size and dropped the other to `r5.large`, since losing one replica entirely felt like too much risk for the savings, but running two identically oversized ones no longer made sense. That was roughly $260/month back.

The bigger win, dollar for dollar, was moving batch jobs to spot instances. We run nightly ETL jobs and a handful of scheduled report-generation tasks that are naturally interruption-tolerant — if a spot instance gets reclaimed mid-job, our job runner just requeues the work, and worst case a report is an hour late, which nobody has ever actually noticed. Those jobs were running on three dedicated `c5.xlarge` on-demand instances kept warm essentially all month for maybe four hours of actual daily use, at roughly $370/month each. We moved them to an EC2 Fleet with a spot allocation strategy and a mix of instance types as fallback, so we're not fighting for capacity on a single instance family, and now pay closer to $95/month combined for the same workload, including the handful of interruptions we've eaten so far, which have cost us nothing beyond a job restarting.

Total before: roughly $14,200/month, blended across compute, storage, and data transfer. Total after: about $7,100/month, with compute specifically down from $9,800 to $3,400. Storage and data transfer didn't change because we didn't touch them this round — that's next quarter's project, probably starting with S3 lifecycle policies we've been ignoring for two years.

None of this required new tooling beyond what CloudWatch already gives you for free, and none of it required taking on meaningful new risk — we kept enough on-demand capacity that a bad spot interruption week wouldn't touch anything user-facing. The main lesson, if there is one, is that "rightsizing" sounds like a boring, obvious first move, and it is, but almost nobody actually does the unglamorous work of pulling three months of utilization data and sitting with it before deciding. We'd been meaning to for over a year.
