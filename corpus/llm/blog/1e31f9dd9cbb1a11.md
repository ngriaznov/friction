# How We Cut Our AWS Bill Nearly in Half Without Cutting Anything Else

For most of last year, our monthly AWS invoice crept upward the way these things do: nobody made a single bad decision, but a dozen small ones piled up until the number stopped looking reasonable. We're a five-person infra team supporting a data platform with a mix of always-on services and nightly batch processing, and by February our bill had settled at a little over $19,000 a month. By June it was just above $10,000. Nothing about our product changed in that window. We just stopped paying for compute we weren't using.

## Step one: actually look at what's running

The first thing we did wasn't clever — we pulled a month of Cost Explorer data broken down by instance type and tagged team, and cross-referenced it against CloudWatch CPU and memory utilization for every EC2 instance in the account. The pattern was almost embarrassing. We had a fleet of `m5.2xlarge` instances running our API tier at a steady 12-15% CPU, sized for a traffic spike that happens twice a year during a seasonal promotion. We had a Postgres read replica on an `r5.xlarge` that peaked at 20% memory utilization. And we had several long-running `c5.4xlarge` boxes dedicated to internal tooling that, it turned out, could run comfortably on a quarter of that capacity.

Rightsizing those alone took real effort but no real risk: we moved the API tier down to `m5.large` with auto scaling picking up the slack during real traffic spikes, dropped the read replica to an `r5.large`, and cut the internal tooling boxes to `c5.xlarge`. That single pass took our EC2 line item from roughly $11,400/month to about $6,200/month — a 46% reduction on compute we were already committed to, achieved by matching instance size to actual load instead of to guesses made eighteen months earlier.

## Step two: batch jobs don't need guarantees

The second and bigger lever was our batch processing fleet. We run nightly ETL jobs, a set of nightly report builds, and periodic reprocessing jobs that can each take anywhere from twenty minutes to four hours. None of these are user-facing, all of them are idempotent and checkpointed, and none of them care if the underlying instance disappears mid-run as long as the job can resume or retry. That's precisely the workload spot instances are built for, and precisely the workload we'd been running on full-price on-demand instances out of an old habit of "batch jobs are stable, let's not touch them."

We moved the batch fleet to a spot-instance strategy using EC2 Fleet with a diversified set of instance types across three availability zones, so a capacity crunch in one pool wouldn't stall the whole job. We added simple checkpointing to the two jobs that didn't already have it — about three days of engineering work total — so an interruption meant a restart from the last checkpoint rather than starting over. Spot pricing on the instance families we use runs 60-70% below on-demand in our region, and in practice we saw our batch compute costs drop from around $4,800/month to about $1,700/month, even accounting for the occasional job that took a retry or two.

## The numbers, roughly

Before: ~$11,400/month EC2 on-demand for steady-state services, ~$4,800/month for batch on-demand, plus another ~$2,900/month in storage, data transfer, and miscellaneous services. Total: around $19,100/month.

After: ~$6,200/month for rightsized steady-state services, ~$1,700/month for spot batch compute, and the same ~$2,900/month in storage and transfer we hadn't touched yet. Total: around $10,800/month.

That's a 44% reduction, done over about six weeks of part-time effort by two engineers, with zero downtime and no customer-visible change in behavior.

## What we'd tell another team starting this

Look at utilization before you look at pricing options — a smaller on-demand instance beats a cleverly discounted oversized one every time. And treat "stable" and "critical" as separate questions: a batch job can be perfectly stable in its logic while still being a great candidate for interruptible, cheaper compute, because stability of the workload and tolerance for interruption aren't the same property. The storage and data-transfer line is next on our list, and we expect a similar story: not a single dramatic fix, just several months of assumptions nobody had gotten around to checking.
