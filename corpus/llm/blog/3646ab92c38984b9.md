When the two of us started building the product last year, one of the first real infrastructure decisions we had to make was the database, and we spent about a week going back and forth between Postgres and MySQL before landing on Postgres. Writing down the actual reasons now, because most of the comparisons I read while deciding were either outdated benchmarks from a decade ago or vague appeals to "which one do you like better," and neither was useful to two people trying to pick something they'd be stuck operating for years.

The single biggest factor for us was JSON handling. Our product ingests semi-structured data from a handful of partner APIs, and the shape of that data changes often enough that a rigid schema for it would mean a migration every few weeks. Postgres's `jsonb` type gives us indexed, queryable JSON columns sitting right next to strongly-typed relational columns for the parts of our data model that are stable — customer records, subscriptions, that kind of thing. MySQL has had a JSON type since 5.7, but it's not indexable the same way without generated columns as a workaround, and the querying ergonomics around `JSON_EXTRACT` felt noticeably clunkier every time I tried to sketch out our actual queries against it. For a startup where the schema of incoming data is still evolving under us, that flexibility mattered more than almost anything else on the list.

The second factor was constraints and correctness guarantees, specifically around concurrent writes. We do a fair amount of upsert-heavy work — reconciling records from those partner feeds — and Postgres's support for partial indexes and expressive `CHECK` constraints let us push more correctness into the database layer instead of application code, which felt safer given that it's two of us and neither of us wants to be debugging a race condition in business logic when we could have had the database reject the bad state outright. MySQL's InnoDB engine is solid and has gotten a lot better over the years, but the specific constraint features we wanted — particularly exclusion constraints for a scheduling feature we knew was coming — just aren't something MySQL offers at all.

On the operational side, we're running managed Postgres on RDS, so a lot of the historical "MySQL is easier to operate" arguments didn't weigh much for us either way — we're not hand-tuning either engine's buffer pool ourselves. But extensions did tip things further toward Postgres. We're using PostGIS for a lightweight geo feature in the product, and having that available as a well-supported extension rather than a bolt-on we'd have to maintain ourselves was a real point in Postgres's favor. We also expect to lean on `pg_cron` for some scheduled maintenance jobs instead of standing up a separate scheduler service, which is the kind of small operational simplification that matters a lot more when there are two of you than it would at a company with a platform team.

What we gave up, as far as we can tell: MySQL's replication tooling has historically been simpler to reason about for basic read-replica setups, and there's a slightly larger pool of engineers who know MySQL specifically if we ever need to hire someone who can hit the ground running on database work. Neither of those outweighed the JSON flexibility and constraint model for the specific shape of data we're dealing with. If our product were a more conventional, stable relational schema from day one — an e-commerce catalog, say — I think this decision would have been much closer, maybe even a coin flip. It wasn't close for us because of what our data actually looks like.
