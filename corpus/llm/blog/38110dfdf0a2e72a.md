# I Wrote a Photo Deduplicator That Ignores File Hashes

My photo library is 94,000 files spread across fifteen years, four phones, two failed Lightroom migrations, and one very bad decision involving a Dropbox "selective sync" checkbox. I've run every off-the-shelf dedup tool I could find at it. They all found about 1,200 duplicates and stopped, satisfied.

The problem is that they were all comparing SHA-256 digests. A byte-identical copy is the easy case. What my library was actually full of was the *hard* case: the same photo saved at three different JPEG quality levels, the same photo with EXIF orientation baked in versus not, the same photo once at full resolution and once as a 2048px export I made for someone's wedding slideshow in 2016. Not one byte of those files matches. To a hashing tool they're four separate photographs. To me they're one photograph and three pieces of garbage.

So I wrote `dupepix`.

## Perceptual hashing, briefly

The idea is old and simple. Instead of hashing the bytes, you hash what the image *looks like*. `dupepix` uses a difference hash: downscale the image to 9×8 grayscale, compare each pixel to its right-hand neighbor, emit a 1 if it's brighter and a 0 if it isn't. That's 64 bits describing the coarse gradient structure of the image.

Re-encode the JPEG at quality 60 and those 64 bits barely move. Resize it to a quarter of its dimensions — barely move. Crop 30% off one side and they move a lot, correctly, because that's a different photo now.

Two images are candidate duplicates when their hashes are within a Hamming distance threshold. Default is 6 out of 64. Below 4 you start missing genuine re-encodes; above 10 you start collapsing photos that merely share a composition, like two shots of the same beach ten minutes apart.

## Making it not be O(n²)

94,000 images means 4.4 billion pairwise comparisons. That's actually fine — a 64-bit XOR and popcount is a couple of nanoseconds, so brute force finishes in a few minutes. I did it anyway with a BK-tree because the naive version made the progress bar sit at 0% for long enough that I assumed it had hung. Sometimes the optimization is for the user's nerves.

The expensive part is decoding 94,000 JPEGs, not comparing them. `dupepix` caches hashes in a SQLite file keyed by path plus mtime plus size, so the second run over the same library takes eleven seconds instead of forty minutes.

## The burst shot problem

Here's where it got interesting. Phones shoot bursts. Ten frames of my nephew blowing out candles, taken over 1.4 seconds, nearly identical composition, nearly identical exposure. Perceptually they hash within 2 or 3 bits of each other.

They are not duplicates. They are ten photographs, and one of them is the one where his eyes are open.

My first version happily proposed deleting nine of them, and I nearly let it. The fix has two parts.

First, `dupepix` groups by capture time before it groups by hash. If a cluster of near-identical images all carry `DateTimeOriginal` values within a configurable window — 3 seconds by default — it's flagged as a burst, not a duplicate set, and moved to a separate report section that defaults to *keep everything*.

Second, for bursts it does something the hash can't: it computes a sharpness score, the variance of the Laplacian over the luminance channel, and sorts the group by it. So the burst report reads "10 frames, 1.4s span, sharpest is IMG_4471.HEIC" and leaves the decision to me. I usually keep two and delete eight, but I do it with my eyes on the thumbnails.

The distinction that took me longest to articulate: exact and perceptual duplicates are a *storage* problem, and bursts are a *curation* problem. Conflating them is how you delete the good frame.

## What it found

31,400 files, 214 GB. Most of it was the 2016 slideshow exports, which I'd apparently made four separate times.

`dupepix` never deletes anything. It writes a JSON manifest and a shell script full of `rm` lines that you're expected to read before running. After the burst incident I don't trust myself, let alone my own software.

Source is on my Git host; it's about 900 lines of Go and one very defensive test suite.
