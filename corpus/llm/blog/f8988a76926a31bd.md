# We moved our Sidekiq queues to SQS, and the first week was rough

We ran Sidekiq on a Redis instance for about four years. It was fine. It was, honestly, better than fine — Sidekiq is a well-built piece of software and I'd recommend it to most teams. What finally pushed us off it wasn't the library. It was the Redis underneath.

## Why we switched

Two things stacked up.

The first was durability. Our Redis was ElastiCache with AOF disabled, because someone had turned it off years earlier for latency reasons and nobody remembered. We found this out during a failover in February, when we lost about eleven minutes of enqueued jobs. Most of them were harmless — cache warmers, thumbnail generation. Some of them were not. We spent the following two days reconstructing a few hundred customer-facing emails from application logs. Turning AOF back on was the obvious fix, and we did, but the incident review kept circling the same question: why are we running a durable work queue on a system we chose for being fast and ephemeral?

The second was capacity. We had one queue — image processing — that could balloon to two million jobs during a bulk import. Redis holds all of that in memory. Twice we had to scale the instance mid-incident because the queue depth was pushing us toward the maxmemory limit, and the eviction policy would have started dropping keys that weren't queue data at all. SQS just... doesn't have this problem. Depth is somebody else's concern.

We evaluated keeping Sidekiq and moving to a dedicated Redis with proper persistence. It would have worked. We went with SQS mostly because our infra team was already carrying a lot of stateful services and wanted to carry one less.

## What broke

**Ordering assumptions we didn't know we had.** Sidekiq's default queue is FIFO in practice. SQS standard queues are not. We found three jobs that quietly depended on running in enqueue order — the worst was a subscription state machine that would occasionally process a cancellation before the upgrade that preceded it. We caught two in staging and one in production, on day three, when a customer got billed for a plan they'd already cancelled.

**Visibility timeout versus job runtime.** Sidekiq jobs run until they finish. SQS gives you a visibility timeout, and if the job outlives it, the message reappears and another worker picks it up. Our default was 30 seconds. Our PDF export job routinely takes four minutes. We got duplicate exports for two days before anyone connected the dots, because the symptom was "customers occasionally receive two copies of the same report," which sounds like an email bug.

**No `perform_in`.** SQS `DelaySeconds` caps at 15 minutes. We had a lot of `perform_in(2.hours)` calls for retry backoff and reminder scheduling. We ended up writing a small scheduler table in Postgres that a cron job drains into SQS. It works, but it's ours to maintain now, and it's the piece I'd most like back.

**Batch APIs.** Sidekiq's `push_bulk` sends a whole array in one round trip. SQS batches at ten messages. Our importer went from one enqueue call to two hundred thousand, and the enqueue itself became a bottleneck until we parallelized it.

## The first week on call

Not great. Ten pages over seven days, against a baseline of two or three.

Most of them were not real outages. They were alerts we hadn't recalibrated: queue-depth thresholds tuned for Redis semantics, a dead-letter queue alarm that fired on the first message rather than a rate, latency dashboards reading a metric that no longer existed. Six of the ten pages were monitoring, not the system.

The other four were the visibility timeout and the ordering bugs above. Both were fixable in an afternoon once identified. Both took longer than that to identify, because the failure modes were unfamiliar and our mental model was still Sidekiq's.

By week three the page count was back under baseline, and it's stayed there. Would I do it again? Yes — but I'd spend a full sprint auditing job runtimes against visibility timeouts *before* the cutover, not after.
