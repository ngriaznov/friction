# We Picked Postgres, and Here's the Boring Reason Why

There are two of us. We had a week to pick a database before the schema work started blocking everything else, and neither of us wanted to spend that week reading benchmark blog posts written by people selling something. So we wrote down what our application actually needed, checked which of those needs each database met without extra machinery, and picked the one that won more rows. It was Postgres, but the margin was smaller than the internet would have you believe, and two of the deciding factors had nothing to do with performance.

## What the app actually does

We're building a scheduling tool for small clinics. The workload is unremarkable: a few hundred writes a minute at peak, lots of range queries over appointment times, and a reporting view that aggregates across a couple of million rows. Nothing here strains either database. If raw throughput had been the deciding factor, we'd have flipped a coin.

## The three things that decided it

**JSONB.** About a third of our data is per-clinic custom fields — intake questions, insurance codes, whatever a given practice wants to track. We knew from the first customer conversation that we couldn't model this as columns. Postgres's `jsonb` type with GIN indexing meant we could store that blob and still query inside it with reasonable speed. MySQL has a JSON type and it's fine, but indexing into it means adding generated columns and indexing those, which is a real thing people do successfully and also a thing we didn't want to maintain with two engineers. This was the single largest factor.

**Transactional DDL.** Postgres lets you wrap schema changes in a transaction and roll them back. MySQL's DDL is implicitly committing, so a migration that fails halfway leaves you in a partial state you have to clean up by hand. With no DBA and no dedicated ops person, "the migration either applies or it doesn't" is worth a lot. We've already had one migration fail in staging and the rollback was a non-event.

**Range types and exclusion constraints.** This one is niche and it delighted us. Preventing double-booked appointments is the core correctness problem in a scheduling app, and Postgres will enforce it in the database with an exclusion constraint over a `tstzrange`. In MySQL we'd have written that check in application code, gotten it right most of the time, and eventually shipped a race condition. Pushing the invariant into the database removed a whole category of future bug.

## What we gave up

MySQL's replication story is simpler and better documented for the common case, and there are more managed hosts with cheap tiers. Connection handling is the other real cost: Postgres's process-per-connection model means we'll need PgBouncer before we need it for anything else, and that's a component MySQL users mostly don't think about. We've budgeted for it.

We also gave up the enormous corpus of MySQL operational advice. When something goes wrong at 2am, the odds that someone has already blogged the exact error are higher with MySQL. That's not nothing.

## The advice

If your data is mostly rectangular and your team has MySQL experience, use MySQL and don't feel bad. Our decision was driven by two specific type-system features and one property of the migration path, not by a general belief that one engine is better. Write down your actual requirements first. The list will probably be shorter than you expect, and it will pick for you.
