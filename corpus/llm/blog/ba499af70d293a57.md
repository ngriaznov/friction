# Moving a fourteen-year-old PHP app off shared hosting

The app is a membership database for a regional cycling federation. It was written in 2011, it runs on PHP, and until last month it lived on a shared host that charged £9 a month and gave me a cPanel login and very little else. The move to a VPS took a weekend. Finding everything the move quietly broke took another three weeks.

## Why bother

Two reasons. The host was still on PHP 7.4 with no upgrade path they were willing to commit to, and I had no way to run anything that wasn't a PHP file in a web root. No queue worker, no Redis, no ability to install a CLI tool. Every background job was a cron entry hitting a URL with `wget`.

I picked a 2 GB VPS from a boring provider, put nginx and PHP-FPM 8.2 on it, and moved the MySQL database over with `mysqldump`. The app itself is old but not exotic — no framework, a lot of `require_once`, a fair amount of `mysqli`. It needed about forty small fixes for PHP 8 (mostly `each()`, a couple of curly-brace string offsets, one place where a class had a constructor named after the class). All of that was findable by turning error reporting up to `E_ALL` and clicking through the site.

## The cron jobs

Here's the part that cost me.

On the old host, the cron entries were defined in cPanel. I had exported the site files and the database. I had not exported the cron table, because I had genuinely forgotten it existed as a separate thing. In my head "the site" meant the files plus the database.

So I cut DNS over on a Sunday evening, everything looked fine, and nothing complained. The web app worked. Members logged in. Nobody noticed.

What stopped happening:

- The nightly job that emailed race organisers their entry lists
- The weekly membership-expiry reminder
- A monthly job that rebuilt a cached standings table
- A daily backup to an off-site bucket

The first three all failed silently because they were *emails that didn't get sent*. Nobody reports an email they never received. The backup job failing was the one that mattered most and the one that would have taken longest to discover, because it only matters on the day you need it.

I found them because a race organiser rang me in week three to ask why she was still getting her entry list as a PDF she had to download manually instead of by email. That prompted me to go looking, and the old host — to their credit — still had the account in a suspended state for 30 days. I logged into cPanel, screenshotted the cron table, and there they all were. Four jobs. All `wget` calls to URLs like `/tasks/nightly.php?key=...`.

## What I changed

I did not recreate them as `wget` calls. Instead:

- Each task became a CLI entry point invoked as `php /srv/app/bin/task.php nightly`, so it can't be triggered by anyone who guesses the URL.
- Every task writes a timestamped line to a log, success or failure.
- A fifth cron job runs each morning, checks that each of the other four has logged a success in its expected window, and emails me if one hasn't. It's about thirty lines of PHP and it's the most valuable thing I wrote all month.

The lesson isn't "remember your cron jobs." It's that a job which only produces side effects elsewhere — an email, a file in a bucket — has no natural failure signal. Something has to actively check that it ran. On shared hosting I'd been getting away without that for a decade, mostly by luck.
