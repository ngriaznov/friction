# Six Months of Pairing Full-Time

Half a year ago our team switched to full-time pair programming — every ticket, every day, two people at one keyboard. It wasn't a trial we eased into; a new lead came in, made the case in a single meeting, and we started the following Monday. Six months on, I want to write down what actually changed, because it's different from what I expected going in.

The most obvious improvement is one nobody argues about: knowledge stopped pooling in individual heads. Before, if you wanted to know why the billing service retried failed webhooks the way it did, you asked the one person who'd written it eight months earlier and hoped they remembered. Now that knowledge spreads by default, because two people touched the code and one of them will still be around when the question comes up. Onboarding got faster for the same reason — a new hire pairs with someone from day one and is contributing to real tickets by the end of week one, instead of reading docs alone for two weeks first.

Code review basically disappeared as a separate step, which was the biggest workflow shift. We still open pull requests, but they're rubber-stamped in minutes because the two authors already argued about every decision while writing it. That moved a huge amount of latency out of our cycle time. The flip side is that we lost the outside perspective a third reviewer used to bring — problems that neither pair member noticed because they were both convinced by the same reasoning in the moment. We've partly compensated with periodic swap-ins where a third person sits in for an hour on something gnarly, but it's not a full substitute.

What got more tiring is harder to talk about honestly. Pairing all day is a genuinely different kind of tired than solo coding — it's constant, low-grade social effort layered on top of the technical work, and it doesn't let up the way solo focus time does, where you can go quiet for twenty minutes and just think. A few people on the team, myself included some weeks, hit a wall by Thursday that we didn't used to hit.

The fix that actually worked was structural, not attitudinal: we stopped pretending a pairing day was an eight-hour pairing day. We moved to a fixed schedule — pairing blocks in the morning and early afternoon, with the last ninety minutes of the day reserved as solo time for whichever person wants to write something alone, catch up on messages, or just decompress before standup the next morning. We also instituted a norm that either partner can call a ten-minute break without needing a reason, and people actually use it now that it's explicit rather than something you have to negotiate in the moment.

Would I go back? Not entirely. The team is measurably better at knowing what's actually in the codebase, and fewer bugs slip through from someone working in isolation on a wrong assumption. But I'd tell any team starting this that the fatigue is real and worth designing around from day one, rather than discovering it the hard way after two months of everyone quietly burning out and not saying so.
