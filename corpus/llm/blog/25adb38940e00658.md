Six months ago our team moved to full-time pair programming — not pairing as an occasional practice for gnarly problems, but as the default way everyone works, rotated across pairs every couple of days. I was skeptical going in. Here's where I've landed.

What got easier first was code review, in the sense that it mostly disappeared as a separate bottleneck. When two people have already argued about a design decision in real time, the pull request stops being the place where disagreements happen and becomes more of a formality — a second pair of eyes from someone who wasn't in the room, catching things neither of the original two noticed, rather than a venue for relitigating architecture. Our average time from PR open to merge dropped from a bit over a day to a few hours, and more importantly the review comments themselves got shorter and less contentious, because the big questions had already been resolved before the diff existed.

Knowledge silos also eroded faster than I expected. We rotate pairs every two days, which sounds almost too rapid, but it means that over the course of a month everyone touches most parts of the codebase with most other people. The scary corners — the billing reconciliation logic, the auth middleware nobody wanted to own — stopped being scary because they stopped being any one person's private domain. When someone goes on vacation now, nothing grinds to a halt waiting for them specifically, which was a real and recurring problem before.

What got more tiring is, unsurprisingly, being on the entire time. Solo work has a kind of low-grade slack built into it — you can stare out the window for two minutes, check a personal email, let your brain idle between thoughts — and pairing removes almost all of that, because someone is watching and, more to the point, someone is depending on you to actually be present. The first month, several of us were reporting a specific flavor of end-of-day fatigue that felt different from a normal hard day, more like social exhaustion than mental exhaustion, even for people on the team who consider themselves fairly extroverted. It wasn't the technical difficulty; it was the sustained, unbroken interpersonal engagement.

The schedule change that actually fixed this, more than anything else we tried, was moving from full eight-hour pairing days to a structure with two ninety-minute pairing blocks in the morning, a solo-focused block after lunch for anything that genuinely benefits from heads-down individual work — writing docs, doing exploratory research, cleaning up your own backlog of small tasks — and then one more pairing block before the end of the day. We also made explicit five-minute breaks between pairing blocks non-optional, which felt almost silly to formalize until we noticed people just weren't taking breaks on their own when nothing forced the transition.

Six months in, I'd say the team overall produces better code, onboards new hires faster — our newest engineer was meaningfully productive within a week, which never happened under our old solo-with-review model — and has fewer of the quiet, unaddressed disagreements about approach that used to surface much later as friction during review. But it took real, deliberate schedule engineering to make it sustainable rather than just switching the format and hoping people would adapt on their own. I don't think we could have kept doing straight eight-hour pairing days past month two without burning people out, and I'm glad we caught that early rather than pushing through it as a point of pride.
