# Moving Our Job Queue from Sidekiq to SQS: What Actually Happened

For three years, every background job in our Rails app ran through Sidekiq backed by Redis. It worked fine until it didn't: our Redis instance was the single point of failure for everything from sending emails to processing payments, and we'd had two incidents in six months where a memory spike on that box took down every queue at once. When our infra lead proposed replacing it with Amazon SQS, the pitch was simple: SQS is a managed service, it scales without us thinking about it, and it removes an entire category of "Redis fell over" pages from our on-call rotation.

The decision wasn't purely about reliability. We were also paying for a fairly large ElastiCache instance mostly so Sidekiq had headroom during traffic spikes, and SQS's pay-per-request pricing looked like it would cost us less for the same volume. Between the operational risk and the cost line, the migration got approved in a single planning meeting, which in hindsight should have been our first warning sign.

## What broke

The core mechanical migration was straightforward: we swapped Sidekiq's client for `aws-sdk-sqs` and wrote a thin adapter so our existing job classes didn't need to change their public interface. The pain showed up in the details Sidekiq had been quietly handling for us. Sidekiq's retry logic uses exponential backoff and dead-letter handling that we'd never had to think about; SQS gives you visibility timeouts and redrive policies instead, and mapping our existing retry semantics onto those primitives took two full days of trial and error. We also discovered that a handful of our jobs assumed strict FIFO ordering, which standard SQS queues don't guarantee. We ended up moving those specific job types to FIFO queues, which have lower throughput limits and forced us to shard one particularly busy job type across multiple queues to avoid throttling.

The other surprise was message size. SQS caps messages at 256KB, and it turned out a few of our report-generation jobs were serializing enough context into the job payload to occasionally exceed that. We fixed it by having those jobs store their payload in S3 and pass a reference instead, which is arguably a better pattern anyway, but it wasn't in the original migration estimate.

## The first week on-call

We cut over on a Thursday afternoon, against our own better judgment about Friday deploys, and the first week was rougher than anyone anticipated. Visibility timeout misconfigurations caused a batch of jobs to be processed twice before we tuned them properly, which surfaced a handful of non-idempotent jobs we didn't know we had. We paged on-call three times in the first four days, all related to queue depth alarms that were tuned for Sidekiq's dashboards and didn't map cleanly to SQS's CloudWatch metrics.

By the second week, things settled. We rewrote our alerting around SQS's own metrics instead of trying to force Sidekiq-shaped dashboards onto it, and the non-idempotent jobs got fixed as they turned up. Six weeks out, on-call load is measurably lower than it was with Sidekiq, and we haven't had a single queue-wide outage since the cutover. The migration took roughly three times longer than we estimated, almost entirely because of retry semantics and message-size edge cases we didn't know existed until we hit them in production. If we did it again, we'd spend a week just cataloging every job's assumptions about ordering, retries, and payload size before writing a line of adapter code.
