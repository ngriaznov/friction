# I built a standup bot, and then it yelled at everyone

Our team is spread across four time zones, so the daily video standup had turned into a meeting that three people attended and two people read about later. I spent a weekend replacing it with a Slack bot.

The design is about as simple as it gets. At 9:00 in each person's local morning, the bot posts one message to `#eng-standup`: yesterday / today / blockers, plus a nudge to reply in the thread. Everyone answers in the thread whenever they get to it. At 5pm the bot posts a short roll-up naming anyone who hasn't replied — not to shame them, just because a thread with eleven replies is hard to scan for absences.

It's a few hundred lines of Python. `slack_bolt` for the API surface, APScheduler for the timing, and a tiny SQLite file mapping Slack user IDs to their timezone and whether they're currently on leave. The thread collection is the only mildly interesting part: `chat.postMessage` returns the `ts` of the message it created, and if you stash that and pass it back as `thread_ts`, replies land in the thread instead of the channel. Reading them back is `conversations.replies` with the same `ts`. That's the whole mechanism.

The part worth writing down is the deploy.

I had a `send_prompt()` function that took a channel and a date, checked SQLite for whether a prompt had already gone out that day, and returned early if it had. Good. What I didn't have was that check running against the *production* database, because my first deploy ran with `DB_PATH` unset and the code fell back to `":memory:"`.

An in-memory SQLite database is empty on every read. So the idempotency check passed, always. And because I had also — for reasons that made sense at 11pm on a Sunday — set the scheduler to poll every sixty seconds rather than fire once, the bot decided it had never posted, and posted, forty-one times, in about forty-one minutes, at 6am, to a channel with our whole engineering org in it. Mobile notifications on.

I woke up to a lot of messages.

Two fixes went in that morning. The obvious one: `DB_PATH` is now required, and the process refuses to start without it rather than picking a friendly default. Falling back to something that "works" is how you get a bug that only shows up in production. The less obvious one: the bot now refuses to send more than three messages to any channel in an hour, no matter what the rest of the logic thinks. A rate limit you can't reason your way past is worth more than a correctness argument you're confident in.

Six months on, participation is better than the video call ever got, and nobody has been woken up since.
