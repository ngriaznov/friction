We ran code review through Gerrit for a little over three years. Last quarter we switched to GitHub pull requests, and enough people have asked me why that I figured it was worth writing up properly instead of explaining it in Slack threads for the tenth time.

The honest answer is that Gerrit was built for a workflow we didn't actually have. It's phenomenal if you've got a large contributor base submitting individual, tightly scoped commits that need to be reviewed and rebased independently of each other, which is more or less the Android and Chromium model it grew up serving. We're five engineers. Our changes are branches with several commits that get squashed on merge, and Gerrit's whole change-per-commit model fought us constantly. Every amended commit created a new patchset, which was correct behavior and also incredibly confusing for anyone who hadn't fully internalized Gerrit's mental model, which after three years was still not everyone on the team.

The things we're going to miss are real, though. Gerrit's diff view against previous patchsets was genuinely better than anything GitHub offers — you could see exactly what changed between your third and fourth revision, not just the current state against main, which meant reviewers who'd already approved most of a change could focus their re-review on what actually moved. GitHub's "compare" view exists but it's clunky enough that in practice nobody used it; people just re-read the whole diff every time, which wastes reviewer time on changes that only fixed a typo. We also lost the strict "every commit must be reviewed" enforcement, which occasionally saved us from someone sneaking an unreviewed fixup into a branch. And Gerrit's voting model — the -2 to +2 scale with a hard block at -2 — gave reviewers a way to signal "this needs discussion" without it reading as a flat rejection, something GitHub's approve/request-changes binary doesn't capture as gracefully.

What got easier is basically everything else. Onboarding new engineers used to include a solid half-day just on how Gerrit worked, because it's genuinely unlike anything most people have used before, and even after that half-day it took weeks for the workflow to feel natural. GitHub PRs needed zero onboarding time; everyone had already used them somewhere. Our CI integration went from a custom Jenkins-to-Gerrit plugin that someone had to babysit to native GitHub Actions status checks that just work. Draft PRs, suggested-change comments that apply with one click, and the ability to review from a phone during an incident are all things we didn't have before and now use constantly. And honestly, a lot of external contributors and consultants we work with periodically already know GitHub, so we stopped losing the first day of every engagement to tooling explanation.

The migration itself took about two weeks, mostly because we had a handful of long-lived Gerrit changes in flight that we had to either land or manually recreate as branches. We didn't try to preserve review history; we just declared a cutover date and moved forward. A few people grumbled for the first month, mostly the two engineers who'd come from a Gerrit-heavy background at a previous job and genuinely preferred it. Everyone's settled in now. I don't think we'd go back, but I also don't think Gerrit was wrong for us three years ago — we were a different shape of team then, doing more independent, atomic commits from more people, and the tool matched. It just stopped matching.
