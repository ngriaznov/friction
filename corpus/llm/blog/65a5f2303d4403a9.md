# A Year of On-Call With Five People

We finished our first full year of a weekly on-call rotation last month. Five backend engineers, one week each, so you're on roughly every fifth week. Here's what I'd tell a team about to start one.

## Five people is the floor, and it feels like it

Every fifth week sounds fine on a calendar. In practice it means on-call is never far enough away to forget about. You come off a shift, get two weeks of real work, then start thinking about the next one. Vacations, conferences, and one person leaving in March all compressed that further — for six weeks we were effectively a four-person rotation, and everyone felt it.

We never found a clever fix. What helped a little was making swaps completely frictionless: change the name in the schedule, tell the channel, done. No approval, no manager in the loop. People swap constantly now and nobody tracks the balance, which turns out to be fine.

## The runbook requirement did the most

Six months in, we made a rule: any alert that pages a human must link to a runbook. If an alert fires and there's no runbook, the person on-call writes one before the shift ends — even a bad one, even three lines.

I expected this to improve mean time to resolution. It did, some. What I didn't expect was how many alerts it killed.

Writing a runbook forces you to answer "what does the responder actually do here?" For a surprising number of alerts, the honest answer was "look at it, decide it's fine, go back to bed." Those alerts got deleted or downgraded to a dashboard. Others turned out to have a fix so mechanical that we automated it instead of documenting it — the disk-cleanup one took an afternoon and removed maybe a page a month.

Numbers, roughly: we were averaging eleven pages a week before the rule, of which maybe three were things a human needed to act on. Six months later we were at four a week, and about two of those were real. Nobody set out to cut alert volume by more than half. It fell out of being made to write down what the response was.

The runbooks themselves are mediocre and I've stopped apologizing for that. Most are a paragraph of context, a couple of queries to run, and a "if this doesn't work, wake up whoever owns X." That's enough. The 3am version of you does not need prose.

## Handoff beat any tooling change

We do a fifteen-minute handoff on Monday mornings. Outgoing person walks through what fired, what they did, and what's still smoldering. It's the highest-value fifteen minutes of the week and it took us four months to start doing it.

Before that, context died with the shift. Someone would silence an alert Thursday night, the silence would expire Sunday, and the next person would get paged by something that had already been diagnosed. That happened enough times that it became a running joke, which is usually the signal you should have fixed something.

## What we'd still change

Severity levels are still a mess. We have three, we use two, and the boundary between them is vibes. I don't have a good answer.

We also never solved the "quiet week" problem — some weeks you get paged nine times and some weeks zero, and the compensation is the same, which people notice. We talked about paying per page and decided the incentives were bad, but I'm not certain that was right.

If you're starting a rotation: do the runbook rule from day one, do the handoff from day one, and don't let the schedule drop below five without saying out loud that it's temporary. The rest you'll figure out by getting it wrong.
