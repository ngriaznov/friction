# A year of not writing code

I took the manager job in March of last year, on the theory that I had already been doing half of it informally and might as well get paid for the other half. That theory was wrong in an interesting way. The half I had been doing informally — unblocking people, reviewing designs, arguing about priorities in Slack — turned out to be the easy half, and it is now maybe fifteen percent of the work. The rest was invisible to me for nine years because it happens in rooms I was never in.

Here is what actually caught me off guard.

## The feedback loop broke

As an engineer I knew whether I'd had a good day by around 4pm. The test suite passed or it didn't. The p99 came down or it didn't. Even on bad weeks, the badness was legible.

Management gives you no such courtesy. I would finish a day of six conversations and genuinely not know whether I had helped anyone. The results of a good one-on-one show up two months later as someone taking on a project they'd have declined in April, and by then you've had forty other conversations and can't attribute anything to anything. For the first quarter I was constantly, low-grade anxious that I was producing nothing. I now think that feeling is just what the job feels like, and the only cure is to keep a written log. Mine is a plain file where I write two or three lines every Friday about what changed on the team that week. Reading it in December was the first real evidence I had that the year hadn't been wasted.

## I kept solving the wrong problem

Someone brings you a problem. You have solved this problem, or one like it, maybe thirty times. Every instinct you have says: tell them the answer, save them two days.

I did this constantly for about four months and it took my most senior engineer telling me, fairly bluntly, that I was making her feel like a typist. She was right. The value of me answering was two days of her time. The cost was that she stopped bringing me problems she hadn't already solved, because by then the only thing I could add was a rubber stamp, and the interesting conversations disappeared from my calendar.

What I do now is ask what they're thinking before I say anything, and about seven times out of ten their plan is fine, or fine enough that the difference between their plan and mine isn't worth the cost of overriding them. The other three times, asking first means the conversation is about tradeoffs rather than about me being right.

## Hiring is most of the job and nobody tells you

I expected hiring to be a seasonal activity. Instead it was the single largest consumer of my calendar for about seven months — writing the role, arguing with the recruiter about which requirements were real, screening, debriefing, chasing candidates who had gone quiet, and then losing two finalists to counteroffers after weeks of work.

Nothing about being a good engineer prepares you for the emotional shape of this. You spend hours on a person and then a thing outside your control happens and it evaporates. The first time we lost a candidate I took it personally for a week. By the fourth I had learned to run the pipeline like an SRE runs capacity: assume loss, keep more in flight than you think you need, don't get attached to any single instance.

## The hard conversations are hard in a boring way

I braced for the dramatic ones — someone underperforming, someone quitting, someone furious about a decision. Those happened, and they were unpleasant, but they were also short and had obvious next steps.

What actually wore me down was the slow accumulation of conversations where there was no good answer and my job was to say so out loud. The promotion that wasn't going to happen this cycle for reasons that had nothing to do with the person's work. The reorg I couldn't explain fully because I only half understood it myself. The project someone loved that we were cancelling. I could not fix any of these, and there is a strong pull to soften them into something more comfortable and slightly untrue. Every time I gave in to that pull, it cost me credibility later, when the truth arrived anyway and I was standing next to it looking like I'd known.

## I miss it, and that's fine

I still open the codebase. I fix small things, mostly on Fridays, mostly documentation and flaky tests, the kind of work nobody minds losing and nobody is blocked on. I am not on the critical path for anything and I've stopped trying to be. The two times I took a real ticket, I sat on it for eight days while my calendar filled up, and somebody had to go around me.

The compensation, when it comes, is a different kind of leverage. Two of the people on my team did the best work of their careers this year, and I did not write a line of it. I did remove the three things that were eating their week, and I did argue successfully for a scope cut that gave them room. That's a much thinner claim to make than "I shipped the thing." It's also, on the evidence of the last twelve months, apparently the job.

Ask me again next March whether I'd go back.
