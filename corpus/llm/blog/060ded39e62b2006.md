# Ten years of Vim, then a Kotlin job

I started the new job in March. The codebase is about 400,000 lines of Kotlin and Java, split across a dozen Gradle modules, and on day two a staff engineer asked what editor I used. I said Vim. He didn't make a face, exactly, but he did say "let me know if you change your mind" in a way that suggested he'd seen this movie before.

I lasted nine days.

The thing that broke me wasn't syntax highlighting or autocomplete — I had both, tuned to my liking, through a language server I'd been running for years. It was call hierarchies. In a Go or Python codebase, "who calls this" is mostly a grep. In this codebase, half the call sites go through an interface with four implementations, two of which are injected by annotation. Grep gives you the interface. The IDE gives you the four implementations, ranked, in a tree you can expand. I sat next to someone for twenty minutes watching them navigate an inheritance chain I'd been reconstructing by hand for two days, and something in me quietly gave up.

The refactoring tools were the second surprise. I had always assumed "rename symbol" was the whole story, and that I could approximate it with `:%s` plus a bit of care. What I hadn't appreciated was the stuff that isn't textual at all: extract interface, change signature and update every caller including the named-argument ones, move a class between modules and have the imports follow it. These are not conveniences. They change which refactors feel affordable, which changes which refactors you actually do.

The debugger, honestly, I could have taken or left. I'd made peace with print statements long ago and I'm not sure that peace was wrong.

What didn't transfer is more interesting to me. I installed the Vim emulation plugin, obviously, and about eighty percent of my editing muscle memory came across intact — `ci"`, `dap`, visual block, the works. The twenty percent that didn't is all *between* buffers. `:b partial-name` was how my hands thought about moving around a project, and the plugin's version of it doesn't feel the same, so I've mostly stopped. I now navigate by clicking a file tree, like a person from before the war.

I also lost my macros. Not the capability — the habit. In Vim, when I saw a repetitive edit, my hands started recording before my brain had decided anything. In the IDE, by the time I remember macros exist, I've already done the edit twelve times manually.

That's the trade, I think. I gave up a very fast editor and got a very good model of my code. On this codebase, that's not close. On my own projects at home, I'm still in the terminal, and I don't expect that to change.
