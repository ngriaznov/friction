# We adopted conventional commits, and the changelog started writing itself

We are a backend team of seven maintaining an internal billing service that a dozen other teams depend on. For years, our release process included a step nobody wanted: someone would scroll through `git log` since the last tag, squint at forty commit messages, and try to reconstruct what had actually changed for our consumers.

That step is gone now. Here is what we changed and what it bought us.

## The messages were the problem

Our commit history was not lazy so much as inconsistent. Some people wrote thorough messages. Some wrote "fix". Two of us used a `[JIRA-123]` prefix; the rest did not. None of it was machine-readable, so the changelog was always a manual translation job, and the translator was whoever drew the short straw that sprint.

The real cost showed up downstream. Twice in a year we shipped a minor version that quietly changed a response field, because the person cutting the release had no way to notice the breaking part buried in a commit called "adjust invoice serializer".

## What conventional commits actually asks for

The format is small enough to explain in a standup:

```
<type>(<optional scope>): <description>

<optional body>

<optional footer>
```

We use `feat`, `fix`, `refactor`, `perf`, `docs`, `test`, and `chore`. Scope is the module — `invoices`, `webhooks`, `auth`. A breaking change gets `!` after the type and a `BREAKING CHANGE:` footer explaining the migration.

So a real commit from last week:

```
feat(webhooks): add retry backoff configurable per endpoint

Endpoints can now set retry_strategy in the registration payload.
Defaults to the previous fixed 30s interval when omitted.
```

That is not more work than what we were writing before. It is the same information with a prefix.

## Automation was the payoff

Once every message parsed, two things became trivial.

**Changelog generation.** A CI job runs on tag creation, walks the commits since the previous tag, groups them by type, and writes a `CHANGELOG.md` section. `feat` becomes Added, `fix` becomes Fixed, `chore` and `test` get dropped entirely. Nobody edits it by hand anymore, and the output is more complete than our manual version ever was, because it cannot forget anything.

**Version bumps.** The same job decides the version. Any `BREAKING CHANGE` footer or `!` marker bumps major. Any `feat` bumps minor. Otherwise it is a patch. The tag is created automatically and the artifact publishes from there.

The second one changed our behaviour more than we expected. When the version number is derived from the commit rather than chosen by a human at release time, you have to think about breakage at the moment you write the code. That is the right moment.

## The friction that remains

We enforce the format with a `commit-msg` hook plus a CI check on the PR title, since we squash-merge. The hook catches most mistakes locally. The CI check catches the people who skipped the hook.

The genuine annoyance is deciding between `fix` and `refactor` when a cleanup incidentally repairs something. Our rule: if a user could have noticed the old behaviour, it is a `fix`. That resolves about ninety percent of arguments.

The other ten percent we settle in review, which is where they belonged all along.

Six months in, none of us wants to go back. It is a small amount of discipline traded for a release process that no longer depends on anyone's memory.
