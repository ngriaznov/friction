We moved our background job queue off Redis-backed Sidekiq and onto Amazon SQS a few months ago, and I've been meaning to write down how it went while I can still remember which parts hurt.

The short version of why: our Redis instance was becoming a single point of failure that scared me more every quarter. We were running a single primary with a replica for failover, and every time we needed to bump the instance size or apply a maintenance patch, there was a window where job processing either stalled or, worse, lost jobs that were mid-flight. We'd also grown to the point where Sidekiq's memory footprint for enqueued jobs was competing with the caching workload we were also running through the same Redis cluster, because of course two teams had quietly decided to share it two years ago and nobody had revisited that. SQS gave us durability guarantees we didn't have to operate ourselves, and since we were already all-in on AWS, it meant one less piece of infrastructure to patch, back up, and page ourselves about.

The migration itself was less about the code and more about the assumptions baked into how we'd used Sidekiq for six years. Sidekiq gives you a lot for free: retries with exponential backoff, a web UI, scheduled jobs, uniqueness constraints, batches. SQS gives you a queue and a promise about at-least-once delivery. Everything else you build yourself or buy from a library. We ended up using Shoryuken as the consumer framework on the Ruby side, which mimics enough of the Sidekiq worker interface that most of our job classes didn't need to change their business logic, just their base class and how they got enqueued.

The first thing that broke was ordering. We had exactly one job type, a billing reconciliation job, that quietly depended on FIFO execution within a customer's job stream. Standard SQS queues don't guarantee order, and we didn't realize how much we'd relied on Sidekiq's mostly-sequential processing until reconciliation started producing double-charges in staging. We ended up moving just that job type to a FIFO queue with per-customer message group IDs, and left everything else on standard queues.

The second thing was idempotency, or our total lack of it. At-least-once delivery on standard SQS queues means duplicate deliveries are a normal event, not an edge case, and Sidekiq's default behavior had let us get lazy about that for years. We spent a solid week auditing every job class for side effects that weren't safe to run twice — mostly things like "increment a counter" or "send exactly one email" — and either added idempotency keys backed by a small Postgres table or restructured the job to be naturally idempotent.

The third thing, and the one that actually caused an incident, was visibility timeout tuning. We copied a default fifteen-minute visibility timeout, which was way too short for our slowest report-generation jobs. Those jobs would get redelivered to a second worker while the first one was still running, and we'd occasionally end up with two workers hammering the same expensive query at once. We fixed it by setting per-queue timeouts based on p99 job duration plus a healthy buffer, and adding a heartbeat extension for anything long-running.

On-call the first week after cutover was rougher than I'd hoped but not catastrophic. We paged three times, all in the first 48 hours: once for the visibility timeout issue above, once because a Shoryuken worker pool wasn't configured with enough concurrency and queues started backing up during a traffic spike, and once for a silly IAM permissions gap on a newly added queue that nobody had granted send access to from one of our internal services. After that first weekend it settled down. By the second week the on-call load was actually lower than it had been on Sidekiq, mostly because we weren't getting paged for Redis memory pressure anymore. Worth the two months it took, though I wouldn't want to do the idempotency audit again anytime soon.
