# One Year After Breaking Up the Monolith

We started splitting our Rails monolith in March of last year. Twelve months and nine services later, it seems fair to write down what actually happened, rather than what we told ourselves would happen.

Some context on scale, because "microservices" means wildly different things at different sizes. We are a payments-adjacent B2B product with about 40 engineers, roughly 900 paying customers, and a peak load that would embarrass a real high-traffic company. The monolith was seven years old, 340,000 lines of Ruby, and one deploy pipeline that took 52 minutes end to end. Nobody was drowning. We were just slow in a way that had become normal.

## Why we did it

The honest reason was deploy contention. With 40 engineers merging into one main branch, we averaged 14 deploys a day, and any red build blocked everyone. A single flaky spec could stall the queue for 90 minutes. We measured it for a quarter: engineers spent about 6% of their working hours waiting on or babysitting the deploy queue.

The reason we wrote in the proposal doc was "scalability and team autonomy," which was mostly dressing. Deploy contention was the problem. Keep that in mind, because it explains most of what follows.

## What actually got faster

**Deploys, obviously.** The billing service deploys in 6 minutes. Notifications deploys in 4. When a team owns its pipeline, a red build is that team's problem, not a company-wide outage of productivity. Deploy frequency roughly tripled for the teams that got their own service, and the wait-time figure dropped to something under 1%.

**Onboarding into a bounded area.** A new engineer joining the notifications team now reads about 11,000 lines of code instead of trying to build a mental model of everything. Our onboarding survey question "how long until you shipped something to production without help" moved from a median of 9 days to 4. That surprised me; I had expected distributed systems overhead to eat the gain.

**Targeted scaling, in exactly one place.** Our PDF statement renderer used to run in the same processes as everything else, and month-end generation would pin CPU and degrade the whole app for about two hours. Now it is its own service with its own autoscaling group, and month-end is a non-event. This is the textbook argument for microservices and it was genuinely true for us — once, for one component.

**Blast radius on bad deploys.** A bad release used to mean the site was down. Now a bad release usually means one capability degrades. We shipped a broken migration to the audit service in October and most customers never noticed.

## What got slower

**Anything that crosses a seam.** Adding a field that a customer sees on an invoice used to be one PR. It is now three PRs across two repos plus a coordinated release order, and if you get the order wrong you ship a 500. We built tooling to help, but the median time to deliver a small cross-cutting feature went up by something like 40%. That number comes from comparing similar tickets before and after, so treat it as a rough shape rather than a measurement.

**Local development.** Running the full stack on a laptop is not really possible anymore. We landed on a shared dev environment plus service stubs, which works, and which somebody has to maintain forever. That is close to a permanent half-headcount of toil that did not exist before.

**Debugging.** A request that used to be one stack trace is now four hops with correlation ids. We adopted distributed tracing in month three, and I would say the tracing setup was the single highest-value piece of infrastructure work of the whole year. Before it existed, incident triage took roughly twice as long as under the monolith. After it existed, we got back to about parity. Not better. Parity.

**Data consistency.** This is the one we underestimated most. In the monolith, an invoice and its ledger entries were written in one transaction. They are now in two services, and we ended up building an outbox pattern with a reconciliation job that runs hourly and pages someone when drift exceeds a threshold. It has fired eleven times. Nine were real bugs. Every one of those bugs was a class of bug that literally could not exist a year ago.

## The things nobody warns you about

Service ownership rots. Two of our nine services were built by a team that has since been reorganized out of existence. Those services now have no clear owner, and "no clear owner" is much worse for a standalone service than for a directory inside a monolith, because there is a deploy pipeline, a set of dashboards, and a dependency upgrade treadmill attached to it.

Also: we now have four different opinions about pagination. In one codebase, inconsistency is visible and someone eventually fixes it. Across nine repos, inconsistency is invisible until a client integrates with two of them.

## Would we do it again?

Partly. Here is the split I would defend.

Extracting the statement renderer: yes, unambiguously. It had a genuinely different scaling profile and a clean interface.

Extracting billing and audit: probably yes. They have real ownership, real boundaries, and the teams are happier.

The other six: I think we should have tried modular boundaries inside the monolith first — enforced module ownership, separate test suites per module, and parallelized CI. Most of our pain was deploy contention, and there was a cheaper fix for deploy contention that we never seriously costed out. We reached for an architectural change to solve what was substantially a build-engineering problem.

If I could hand our March self one sentence, it would be this: figure out precisely which problem you are solving, then ask what the cheapest thing that solves it is. The architecture diagram is much more fun to draw than the CI parallelization ticket. That is not a good reason to pick it.

We are not going back. Nine services is where we stop, and we have said no to three proposed extractions since December, which I count as the healthiest outcome of the whole year.
