# Six months of pairing full time

We started pairing in January, all seven of us, on a rotation that reshuffled every Monday. Not "pair when it's tricky" — pairing as the default way work got done. Six months in, here's what I'd tell a team considering the same thing.

## What got easier

Onboarding, obviously. We hired two people in March, and neither of them spent a week reading code alone hoping it would make sense. They were in the codebase on day two, driving, with someone next to them who could say "that queue is a trap, everything in there is retried twice." The tribal knowledge that usually sits in three people's heads got distributed almost by accident.

Code review basically stopped being a bottleneck. Not because we skipped it — we still open PRs — but because the review was happening continuously, and the PRs arrived pre-argued. The median time from "PR open" to "merged" went from about a day and a half to under two hours. Nobody planned that. It just fell out.

The thing I didn't expect: fewer half-finished branches. When you're alone and you hit something ugly at 4pm, it's very easy to tell yourself you'll pick it up tomorrow, and then not. With someone else there, you finish the thought. Our count of stale branches older than two weeks dropped from thirty-something to about four.

## What got harder

It's tiring in a way I genuinely did not anticipate. Solo work has natural slack in it — you check something, you stare out the window, you read a doc for ten minutes. Pairing removes almost all of that. Six focused hours of pairing left me more drained than nine hours of solo work ever had, and I wasn't the only one saying so. Two people described it as feeling "socially expensive" even when they liked their partner.

The second problem was quieter. Some kinds of work are just bad in a pair. Sifting through a hundred lines of log output. Renaming things across forty files. Reading a long RFC. We spent weeks pairing on things where the second person was effectively watching someone else scroll, and it took us a while to admit that out loud rather than treat it as a personal failing.

## What we changed

Two adjustments, both about a month apart, and both helped.

First, we capped pairing at five hours a day, with a hard stop. Mornings 10 to 1, afternoons 2 to 4. Outside those windows you're on your own — email, reading, the log-scrolling work, whatever. Cutting an hour of pairing did not cost us an hour of output.

Second, we made "let's split this" a normal thing to say, with no justification required. If either person feels the work has gone solo-shaped, you split and regroup later. Making it a no-questions move meant people actually used it, instead of quietly resenting the session.

## Would I keep doing it

Yes, with the guardrails. The version we ran in January — all day, every day, no escape hatch — would have burned the team out by summer. The version we run now feels sustainable, and I'd be reluctant to go back to a team where nobody pairs.

Give it a full quarter before you judge it. The first six weeks are all cost.
