# I run my own mail server now, and mostly I regret it

I moved my personal email off a hosted provider in March. Six months in, it works. Getting there took a weekend I expected and then about three more weekends I did not.

Here is what I actually did, and where it hurt.

## The box

A $6/month VPS, 1 GB of RAM, Debian stable. Mail is not a heavy workload for one person — the constraint is not CPU, it is having a static IP with clean reputation and working reverse DNS. I checked both before paying: my provider's IP block was not on Spamhaus, and they let me set a PTR record from the control panel. If your provider will not set reverse DNS, stop there. Nothing else you do will matter.

Postfix handles SMTP, Dovecot handles IMAP and local delivery. That split confused me for the first day. Postfix accepts mail from the outside world and hands it off; Dovecot stores it and serves it to my client. They talk over a Unix socket via LMTP, which I configured in `master.cf`:

```
mailbox_transport = lmtp:unix:private/dovecot-lmtp
```

I let Dovecot own the mailbox format entirely (Maildir under `/var/mail/vhosts/`) rather than having Postfix write files directly. That one decision made sieve filtering and the spam training setup much less painful later.

## TLS and authentication

Certificates from Let's Encrypt via certbot, in standalone mode with a deploy hook that reloads both daemons. Postfix gets `smtpd_tls_security_level = may` for inbound from other servers — you cannot demand TLS from strangers — but submission on port 587 is `encrypt` and requires SASL auth against Dovecot. Port 25 does not accept authenticated relay at all. If you get this wrong you become an open relay, and you will find out when your queue has forty thousand messages in it.

Dovecot's auth is against a passwd-file with bcrypt hashes. No system accounts, no shell users. There is exactly one virtual mailbox and it is mine.

## Spam filtering

I started with SpamAssassin and switched to rspamd after two weeks. Rspamd is faster, has a web UI that tells you why something scored what it did, and does DKIM signing in the same daemon, which meant deleting opendkim entirely.

Postfix talks to it via milter:

```
smtpd_milters = inet:localhost:11332
non_smtpd_milters = inet:localhost:11332
```

Training matters more than configuration. I wired two sieve rules: moving a message to Junk feeds it to rspamd as spam, moving it out feeds it as ham. After about three weeks of me being diligent about this, false positives went to roughly zero. Before that, it ate a bank notification and a job-related email, which is exactly the failure mode that makes people go back to Gmail.

Greylisting is on. It delays first contact from unknown senders by five minutes and kills a large fraction of junk with no false positives, at the cost of confusing you the first time a password reset takes six minutes to arrive.

## Deliverability, or: the part that took three weekends

Receiving mail was easy. Sending it to people who use big providers was not.

SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are table stakes. I set all three on day one and still got filed to spam by Outlook. Specifically:

**Microsoft** was the worst by a wide margin. Mail to outlook.com and hotmail.com addresses went to junk for weeks with no diagnostic signal. What eventually fixed it: enrolling in their Smart Network Data Services program, and separately filing a deliverability support request through their web form. I got a form response, then a second form response, and then about ten days later mail started landing in inboxes. I do not know which of those things worked. Possibly neither and it was just age.

**Gmail** was fine after DKIM was verified, with one exception — my first few messages went to spam until there was any reply history. Once someone replies to you once, you are effectively whitelisted for that recipient. Google's Postmaster Tools showed me a domain reputation of "unknown" for about a month, then "high," and I never had to talk to a human.

**Yahoo** never gave me trouble at all, which surprised me.

The general lesson: a brand-new domain sending its first mail from a residential-adjacent IP block looks exactly like a spammer, because that is what spammers look like. There is no configuration that shortcuts this. You send small volumes of legitimate mail for a month and reputation accrues.

## Would I do it again

For a personal domain with one user and low volume — yes, grudgingly. Backups are `rsync` of a Maildir tree, which is genuinely nicer than any provider's export. Nothing scans my mail. I control the filters.

But I check a monitoring dashboard for certificate expiry and disk space, and I have a documented recovery procedure, because if the box dies while I am on vacation, mail bounces. A hosted provider absorbs that risk for a few dollars a month and I now understand exactly what those dollars buy.

If you want to run mail for a team, or for customers, pay someone. The failure modes are not worth it.
