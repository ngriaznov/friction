# Picking Our First Production Database: Postgres vs. MySQL

When it came time to pick a database for our startup, there were exactly two of us, no ops team, and no appetite for a decision we'd have to revisit in a year. We spent about a week going back and forth between Postgres and MySQL before landing on Postgres, and the reasons ended up being narrower and more practical than the general "Postgres is more powerful" arguments you see online.

## The features that actually mattered

Our product leans heavily on modeling relationships between entities that don't fit neatly into rigid tables — user-generated form data, nested preferences, event payloads from third-party webhooks. Postgres's native `jsonb` type, with indexable fields and operators for querying inside the JSON itself, meant we could store that data without standing up a separate document store. MySQL has had JSON support for years, but the querying and indexing story around it never felt as first-class, and we didn't want to be the ones discovering the rough edges in production.

The other deciding feature was partial indexes. A big chunk of our queries only ever care about active, non-deleted rows, and Postgres lets us index just that subset instead of the whole table. It's a small thing, but on our busiest table it kept an index a fraction of the size it would otherwise be, which mattered more than we expected once row counts started climbing.

## What we gave up

MySQL's replication tooling is genuinely more mature out of the box, and we know several small teams who leaned on that heavily as they scaled reads. We also gave up some of the operational simplicity that comes with MySQL's smaller memory footprint — Postgres wants more RAM to be happy, and on the small managed instance we started with, we had to tune `shared_buffers` and `work_mem` sooner than we would have liked. None of this was a dealbreaker, but it wasn't free either.

We also didn't have anyone on the team with deep MySQL operational experience, and only mild Postgres experience, so this wasn't a case of playing to existing strengths. It came down to the managed hosting options available to us (both had solid ones), the JSON and indexing behavior we could already picture ourselves needing in month two, and the fact that Postgres's `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` output has been more legible for the two of us as we've each had to debug slow queries solo, without a DBA to lean on.

## Where we landed

Six months in, the decision has mostly faded into the background, which is what you want from infrastructure choices. The `jsonb` columns turned out to be exactly as useful as we hoped for the webhook payload data, and we haven't hit a wall that made us regret not choosing MySQL. If our access patterns had leaned more toward simple, high-volume read replication with less need for semi-structured data, MySQL likely would have been the better call. For where we actually ended up building, Postgres fit the specific shape of our data better than the generic reputation either database carries.
