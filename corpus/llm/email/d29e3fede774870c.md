**Subject:** Engineering survey results, and what we're doing about them

All,

The engineering satisfaction survey closed three weeks ago. 214 of 247 of you responded — 87%, up from 71% last time. I read every free-text comment, which took most of two evenings and was worth it. This email is long because you gave us a lot to work with and I'd rather be specific than brief.

Full results, including the per-org breakdowns and the anonymised comment themes, are in the "Eng Survey — Spring" folder on the wiki. Nothing is being withheld except comments that would identify individuals.

## The headline

Overall satisfaction came in at 6.8 out of 10, essentially flat from 6.9 last time. Two things moved: "I have clarity on what my team is working on" rose from 6.1 to 7.4, and "I can get help when I'm stuck" stayed high at 8.2. Those are worth noting because they were the things we worked on last year, and the work landed.

Three areas got materially worse or stayed bad. I'm going to take them in the order you ranked them.

## 1. On-call load

This was the single most common theme, appearing in 96 free-text responses. The quantitative score — "on-call is sustainable for me" — was 4.1, down from 5.0.

The data backs you up. I pulled paging volume for the last two quarters: median pages per on-call week is 11, but the distribution is brutal. Four rotations account for 62% of all pages. If you're on Payments Infra or Ingest, your median week is 26 pages. If you're on Platform Tools, it's 2. We have been telling ourselves we have an on-call problem when what we have is four teams with an on-call problem and everyone else absorbing the reputational damage.

Also, 38% of pages in the sample were for alerts that resolved themselves without human action. That's not on-call. That's a pager that cried wolf 900 times last quarter.

What we're doing:

- **Alert bankruptcy on the four worst rotations.** Starting the week of the 14th, each of those teams gets two full sprints with no feature commitments to audit every alert and delete or downgrade anything that isn't customer-impacting and actionable. Their PMs know. This is not a stretch goal on top of the roadmap; the roadmap moves.
- **A hard page budget.** From next quarter, any rotation exceeding 15 pages per week for three consecutive weeks triggers an automatic review with me and the team's director, and the team's next planning cycle prioritises reliability work until it's back under. No exceptions, no arguing about whether this quarter is special.
- **Compensation for out-of-hours pages** is being reviewed by People Ops. I don't have an answer yet and I'm not going to pretend I do. I'll have one by the end of next month, and I'll tell you either way.

What I'm not doing: adding people to rotations to spread the pain. That treats the symptom.

## 2. Tooling friction

Score: 4.6. The comments were remarkably consistent. Local environment setup, CI wait times, and the deploy pipeline came up over and over.

The number that got my attention: median CI time to a green build is 34 minutes, and the p90 is 71. Several of you described batching changes to avoid waiting, which is exactly the behaviour that makes debugging harder later. We are paying for slow CI twice.

What we're doing:

- **A standing Developer Experience team, six engineers, staffed by the end of next month.** Not a rotation, not a 20% commitment. Permanent, with its own roadmap and its own on-call. Hiring internally first; if you want in, talk to your manager or to me directly.
- **Their first two targets are explicit:** p90 CI under 20 minutes, and a from-scratch local environment setup under 30 minutes on a clean laptop. Both are measured, both are published on the dashboard below.
- **Deploy pipeline consolidation.** We currently run three deployment systems for historical reasons that nobody defends anymore. We're going to one by end of Q4. This will be disruptive for some teams and I'd rather say that now than surprise you in October.

## 3. Promotion clarity

Score: 4.9, and the free-text comments here were the sharpest of the survey. The most common version of the complaint, paraphrased: "I don't know what would get me promoted, and I suspect the answer is being on the right project."

That's fair, and partly true, and it's my responsibility.

What we're doing:

- **The career ladder is being rewritten**, not tweaked. The current one describes behaviours at a level of abstraction that makes calibration a matter of taste. The new one will include worked examples at every level for every track — real anonymised scenarios, not adjectives. Draft goes out for comment in six weeks.
- **Promotion calibration notes go to candidates.** Starting with the next cycle, if you're put up and not promoted, you get written feedback on what specifically was missing, agreed in the calibration room rather than reconstructed by your manager afterwards.
- **Publishing promotion rates by org and by level**, aggregated enough to protect individuals. If some parts of the org are meaningfully easier to get promoted in than others, you should be able to see that, and so should I.

## How to follow this

I'm not going to ask you to trust that this happens. There's a dashboard at go/eng-survey-actions with every commitment above, its owner, its target date, and its current status. It updates weekly and it will show red when things slip, because some of them will.

I'll give a written update at the end of each quarter for the next two quarters, and we'll rerun the survey in six months. If the on-call number hasn't moved by then, we did the wrong things and I'll say so.

Two asks. First: if you disagree with how I've read the results, tell me — my calendar has open slots on Thursday afternoons and they're genuinely open. Second: your manager will be discussing your team's specific results with you over the next two weeks. Those conversations are more useful than this email.

Thanks for the honesty. The comments were not comfortable reading, which is how I know they were worth collecting.

Priya
