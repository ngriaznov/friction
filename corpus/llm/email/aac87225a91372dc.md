**Last Month's Star Party**

Our star party out at Miller Field on the 12th turned out to be one of our better-attended events in recent memory — we counted 34 people signed in, including nine first-timers who found us through the library's community board flyer. Conditions were about as good as we get around here: clear skies, low humidity, and a new moon that didn't rise until well after most folks had packed up. Jupiter was the star of the show for the newcomers, with the Great Red Spot nicely placed and four moons clearly visible even in the smaller scopes. Thanks to everyone who brought equipment and, especially, to everyone who spent time letting kids and first-timers look through their eyepieces. That's genuinely how this club grows.

A few other highlights from the night worth mentioning: Carl brought out his 10-inch Dobsonian and spent a solid two hours parked on the Ring Nebula, giving anyone who wandered by a look at one of the better planetary nebulae visible this time of year. We also had a table set up with star charts and a couple of loaner red-light flashlights for anyone who showed up without one — a small thing, but it seems to genuinely help newcomers feel less lost standing around in the dark not knowing what they're allowed to touch. If you have red-light flashlights sitting unused at home, we could always use a few more for the loaner table; just bring them to the next meetup.

One logistics note for future star parties: parking at Miller Field filled up faster than usual this time, so if you're planning to attend the deep-sky night later this month, consider carpooling or arriving a bit earlier than you might otherwise. We're looking into whether the overflow lot near the ranger station can be opened up for club events going forward.

**Member Spotlight: Photographing the Orion Nebula**

This month's member report comes from Dana, who recently upgraded to a new CMOS camera and pointed it at the Orion Nebula for her first serious deep-sky imaging attempt. In her words:

"I'd been shooting with a modified DSLR for two years and finally made the jump to a dedicated CMOS astro camera this spring. The difference in the Orion Nebula shots was honestly bigger than I expected. The read noise is so much lower that I was able to get usable data in much shorter subexposures — I ended up stacking about ninety 60-second frames instead of the twenty 4-minute frames I'd have needed with the DSLR, and that made a huge difference in tracking accuracy since any minor guiding hiccup only cost me a minute instead of four.

The other thing I noticed immediately was how much more the sensor pulled out of the fainter outer wisps of nebulosity — the parts that always looked murky and noisy in my old images came through with real structure this time. I did have to relearn my whole calibration routine (darks, flats, and bias frames all behave a little differently with this sensor), and I'd recommend anyone making this jump budget an extra clear night just for that before attempting a target you actually care about.

Happy to bring my laptop to the next meeting and walk anyone through my processing workflow in PixInsight if there's interest."

If you want to see Dana's final image, she's posted it in the club's photo album — worth a look. She's also offered to bring a couple of prints to the next meeting for anyone who'd rather see it without squinting at a phone screen, and mentioned she's happy to talk through her filter setup one-on-one for anyone considering narrowband imaging from a light-polluted backyard, which is the situation most of us are actually working with.

**Upcoming Guest Lecture: Exoplanet Detection**

We're thrilled to announce that Dr. Elena Marsh, an astrophysicist from the state university's planetary science program, will be joining us next month for a talk on exoplanet detection methods. Dr. Marsh's research focuses on transit photometry, and she'll be covering how amateur-grade equipment — including gear many of us already own — can meaningfully contribute to exoplanet follow-up observations through citizen science networks. This is a rare chance to hear from an active researcher about work that's genuinely within reach of a serious hobbyist. The talk will be held at the community center, with details on RSVP and parking to follow in a separate email closer to the date.
