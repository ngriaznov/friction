## Star party at Hollis Ridge: 63 people, and we ran out of parking

Our February 8th star party was the best-attended event we've held since the 2024 eclipse. Sixty-three signed in at the gate, and Ken counted at least eight more who wandered up from the campground without signing anything. Fourteen scopes were on the field. The seeing was mediocre early — that haze sitting over the valley didn't clear until nearly ten — but from ten until about two in the morning it was the steadiest air I've observed from that site in years.

A few things worth noting for next time. First, the overflow lot filled by 6:40 p.m., which meant a dozen cars parked along the access road with headlights sweeping the field every time somebody arrived late. That's on us for not planning for the turnout. For the March event we'll open the lower field for parking and put a volunteer at the gate until 8 p.m. to direct people in. If you can take a 45-minute shift on the gate, reply to this email and I'll put you on the list.

Second, the public outreach side went well beyond what we expected. Priya and Marcus between them ran the two 8-inch Dobs as public scopes for four straight hours, and the line for Jupiter never really went away. If you have a scope you're willing to point at something bright and let strangers look through, that's genuinely the highest-value thing anyone does at these events. You don't need a fancy instrument. The most common thing we heard all night was somebody being surprised that the moons of Jupiter are visible at all.

Third — and I say this gently — we had a couple of people arrive with white flashlights and phone screens at full brightness. Nobody was upset about it, and the whole point of a public event is that newcomers won't know the etiquette yet. But if you're bringing a friend, a thirty-second heads-up in the car about red lights saves everyone's dark adaptation.

## Member report: Dana Whitfield on the Orion Nebula with a new CMOS camera

Dana sent along a write-up of her first serious run with the ASI533 she picked up in December, replacing the DSLR she'd been using for the past six years. She's kindly let me summarize it here, and the full version with the images is posted on the members' page.

The short version: 3 hours and 40 minutes of total integration on M42 from her back garden in Fairhaven, shot in 60-second and 15-second subs, on a 6-inch f/5 newtonian. The two exposure lengths were the interesting part. The Trapezium region is so much brighter than the surrounding nebulosity that a single exposure length either blows out the core or leaves the outer wings buried in noise. Dana shot both and blended them in post — the long subs for the faint outer structure, the short ones layered in to recover the four stars at the center. She notes this is standard practice for M42 specifically and that she'd read about it for years without trying it, and that it took about twenty minutes in the stacking software once she stopped being intimidated by it.

Her observations on the camera itself: the cooled sensor made a bigger difference than she expected, mostly in how few dark frames she needed compared to the DSLR, where thermal noise varied over the course of a session. She also found the smaller pixels oversampled her focal length slightly and ended up binning 2x2 for the faint stuff. Her conclusion, which I think is worth repeating to anyone considering a similar upgrade: the camera didn't make her images better on its own, it made the processing far more forgiving.

If you've been on the fence about moving from a DSLR to a dedicated astro camera, Dana has offered to talk anyone through her setup at the next meeting.

## Guest lecture: Dr. Amara Osei on exoplanet detection — Thursday, March 20

We're very pleased to announce that Dr. Amara Osei of the Coldwell Observatory will be speaking at our March general meeting. Her talk is titled *"Finding Worlds in the Noise: Twenty Years of Transit Photometry."*

Dr. Osei works on transit timing variations — essentially, using the slight irregularities in when a planet crosses its star to infer the presence of additional planets you can't see directly. She's promised to keep the math accessible and has said she's happy to take questions on amateur exoplanet photometry, which several of our members have been experimenting with using the club's 11-inch.

**Thursday, March 20, 7:30 p.m., Fairhaven Community Center, Room B.** Doors at 7:00. The talk is free and open to the public, so bring people. We'll have coffee and, if Ellen's schedule holds, her lemon cake.
