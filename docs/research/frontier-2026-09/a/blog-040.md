# How I Got My Skeptical Manager to Say Yes to Permanent Remote Work

When my partner took a job three hours away, I had two choices. I could quit, or I could convince my manager, Dave, to let me work remotely for good. Dave is a good manager, but he's openly an office person. He once told our team that "the best ideas happen at the whiteboard."

It took about six weeks, but he said yes. Here's how it went.

## I asked for a conversation, not a decision

I didn't open with "I'm moving, so I need to go remote." I told him my situation and asked what would worry him about me working remotely full-time. Then I let him talk and took notes. I wanted his concerns on the table before I tried to answer any of them.

He raised four.

## Concern 1: "I won't know what you're working on."

This was the biggest one, and it was fair. Dave's sense of how things were going came mostly from walking past desks.

**What I did:** I offered to send a short written update every Friday covering what shipped, what's blocked, and what's next. It took me about fifteen minutes a week. After a month, Dave admitted he knew more about my work than about the work of people sitting ten feet away from him.

## Concern 2: "You'll miss the hallway conversations."

**What I did:** I agreed this was a real cost and didn't pretend otherwise. I suggested two changes. First, I would come to the office for two days every month, timed around sprint planning. Second, the team would move its technical discussions into a shared channel instead of holding them at someone's desk. The second change ended up helping the two teammates who worked part-time as much as it helped me.

## Concern 3: "Others will want the same deal."

This was the concern he was least comfortable saying out loud, and I think it was the one that actually mattered to him.

**What I did:** I suggested we treat my arrangement as a documented trial with clear criteria, not a special favor. If it worked, he'd have a template for evaluating other requests on their merits. If it didn't, he'd have a clean way to end it. Framed that way, it stopped being a precedent problem and became a policy he controlled.

## Concern 4: "What if your performance drops?"

**What I did:** I proposed a 90-day trial with specific measures we both agreed on: delivery against sprint commitments, response time during core hours (10 to 4 in his time zone), and a check-in at 30, 60, and 90 days. If I missed the bar, we'd talk about it openly.

## How it ended

At the 90-day review, Dave mostly talked about the Friday updates. He had started asking the rest of the team to send them too.

Looking back, I think what worked was answering each concern with something he could check, not with promises. I didn't ask him to trust me. I gave him a way to verify it.

It's been fourteen months. I still come in twice a month, and I still send the updates.
