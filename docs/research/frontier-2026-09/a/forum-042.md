**Q: My great-grandfather came through Ellis Island around 1910. How do I find his immigration records?**

---

**A:**

You're in luck. 1910 falls in about the best-documented period of US immigration. Passenger manifests are detailed, and the naturalization process was standardized in 1906, so later papers usually cross-reference the arrival. Here's how to approach it.

### 1. Gather what you already know first

Before you search the manifests, pull whatever you can from US records:

- **1910, 1920 and 1930 censuses.** These ask for year of immigration and naturalization status (AL = alien, PA = first papers filed, NA = naturalized).
- **WWI draft registration cards** (1917–18). These give birth date, birthplace, and sometimes citizenship status.
- Death certificates, obituaries, and marriage records, which may name parents and a hometown.

A year of arrival and a hometown will narrow the manifest search enormously.

### 2. Searching the passenger manifests

The main free sources:

- **The Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation database** (heritage.statueofliberty.org). This is free with registration and shows the original manifest images.
- **FamilySearch**, which is free and has its own separate index of the same manifests.
- **Steve Morse's One-Step search tools** (stevemorse.org). These are a front end to the Ellis Island data that lets you search by partial names, sound-alike names, age range, town, and ship. For difficult names they're far more flexible than the official search form.
- **Ancestry**, which is paid and has yet another independent index.

Each site transcribed the handwriting separately, so a name missing from one index can turn up in another.

Around 1910 the manifests run across **two facing pages** with about 30 columns. You'll typically find:

- Name, age, sex, marital status, occupation
- Nationality, "race or people," and last residence
- **Name and address of the nearest relative in the home country**
- Final destination, and **the name and address of the relative or friend they were joining in the US**
- Who paid for the passage, how much money they carried, and whether they had been in the US before
- Physical description (height, complexion, hair, eyes) and **birthplace (country and town)**

Make sure you open **both pages**. Many indexes link only to the first page, and the second holds the birthplace and the US contact.

### 3. Naturalization records

If he naturalized after September 1906, look for:

- **Declaration of Intention** ("first papers"), filed at least two years before the petition.
- **Petition for Naturalization** ("second papers"), which typically records:
  - Full name, address, and occupation
  - Date and place of birth
  - **Date of emigration, port of departure, ship name, and date and port of arrival**
  - Spouse's name and birth date/place; children's names, birth dates, and residences
  - Date of the declaration and the court where it was filed
  - Witnesses
  - Often an attached **Certificate of Arrival**, in which the government verified the manifest entry

The records are held by the court that handled them: federal district courts (now at the National Archives regional facilities) or county/state courts. Many are indexed on FamilySearch and Ancestry. The **USCIS Genealogy Program** can supply duplicate copies (C-files) for naturalizations from 1906 onward, but it charges fees and has a backlog.

### 4. Name-spelling pitfalls

- **Names were not changed at Ellis Island.** The manifest was written at the *port of departure*, usually by a shipping clerk from the passenger's own documents or speech. Inspectors checked names against that list. Name changes usually happened later, informally or at naturalization.
- **Look for the original, native form**: Giuseppe rather than Joseph, Wojciech rather than Albert, Yitzchak rather than Isaac.
- **Transliteration varies** for names written originally in Cyrillic, Hebrew, or Greek script: Schwartz / Szwarc / Švarc, Kowalski / Kovalsky.
- **Indexing errors are common.** Old handwriting gets misread: a looped "L" read as "S", "n" and "u" swapped, and so on. Search on the first few letters with wildcards and use sound-alike (Soundex or Daitch–Mokotoff) options.
- **Search by other fields.** Try age range plus town of origin, or search for a traveling companion or the relative he was joining, then scan nearby lines on the same page.
- Married women often travelled under their **maiden name**, and children may be listed under the mother.

Once you find the manifest, the hometown and the relative's name are your bridge to records in the old country.
