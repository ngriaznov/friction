# The First Ninety Days of Feeling Like a Fraud

For four years I was one of nine engineers at a startup. I had written a large share of the backend, I knew where every skeleton was buried, and when something broke people came to me.

Then I joined a big, famous tech company, the kind of name that makes your relatives nod approvingly at dinner. For about three months I was sure someone had made a hiring mistake and would figure it out soon.

## What it felt like

It wasn't one big moment. It was a lot of small ones.

In my first design review, people used six acronyms I had never heard, and I nodded along to every one. My first code review had forty-one comments, most of them about internal conventions I didn't know existed. It took me two weeks to get a change into production that would have taken twenty minutes at the startup, and I assumed the delay was my fault.

Worst of all, everyone around me seemed calm. They moved through internal tools I couldn't even find. I took that calm to mean competence and my confusion to mean I didn't belong.

At the startup, my value came from knowing things. At the new job I knew almost nothing, so I assumed I was worth almost nothing.

## What helped

**My manager named it first.** In our third one-on-one she said, without me bringing it up, "Most people from startups feel slow here for about a quarter. It's the environment, not you." Hearing that it was a known pattern with a rough timeline made it feel like a phase rather than a verdict.

**I started keeping a "didn't know, now know" doc.** Every day I wrote down one thing I'd learned: what an acronym meant, how the deploy pipeline worked, who owned which service. By week eight the doc was eleven pages long. On bad days I read it back to myself. It was plain evidence that I was learning, and faster than I felt I was.

**I asked a senior engineer how long her first change took.** She laughed and said three weeks. That one conversation did more for me than any article about imposter syndrome. The people who seemed calm had simply been there longer.

**I found something the startup had taught me that they needed.** Around month two, our team hit a problem with a flaky integration with a third-party API. I had fought almost exactly that fight at my old job, retries and idempotency keys and all. I wrote up an approach, and it shipped. It was a small thing, but it reminded me that my old experience still counted. It was just filed under a different heading.

**I stopped treating code review comments as grades.** Once I read the forty-one comments as a free tour of the codebase's conventions and not as a list of my failures, reviews got a lot less painful. My most recent change got three comments.

## Where I am now

The feeling hasn't completely gone away. It still comes back when I get pulled into an unfamiliar area. But it's quieter now, and it passes faster.

If you've just made the same jump, my advice is to write down what you learn every day, ask people how long things took them, and give it a quarter before you decide anything about yourself.
