For an arrival around 1910, you're in luck: Ellis Island was operating at full volume and the manifests from this period are detailed, indexed and online. The main difficulty is not the records but the indexing, so I'll cover that last.

## Passenger manifests

Ship manifests (officially "Lists or Manifests of Alien Passengers") for New York arrivals 1892–1924 are indexed at three main places:

- **Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation** (heritage.statueofliberty.org): free, with manifest images. The search is finicky but the "close match" options are useful.
- **FamilySearch**: free, same underlying NARA microfilm (T715), and its index is independent, so a name mis-transcribed on one site may be right on the other.
- **Ancestry** ("New York, Passenger Lists, 1820–1957"): paid, best browse interface, and lets you page through the whole ship.

Search with the widest net first: surname only, arrival year ±2, approximate birth year ±5, country of origin. Then narrow.

A 1910 manifest is two pages wide. The left page has name, age, sex, marital status, occupation, literacy, nationality, "race or people", and last permanent residence (town and country). The right page has the name and address of the nearest relative in the old country, the final destination in the US, whether they had a ticket to it, who paid the passage, and the name and address of the relative or friend they were joining. Those last two columns are gold: they frequently name a brother or cousin already in the US, with a street address.

Always look at the image, not just the index. Also check the **left margin** for handwritten notations like "SI" (held for Special Inquiry) or a certificate number. A stamped number and date in the margin, added years later, usually means the record was verified for a later naturalization, which leads to the next section.

## Naturalization petition

After 1906, naturalization became a federal process with standard forms, so anything filed by your great-grandparent from about 1912 onward will be consistent. The process had two parts:

1. **Declaration of Intention** ("first papers"), filed after a couple of years in the US.
2. **Petition for Naturalization** ("second papers"), usually five or more years after arrival.

The petition typically records: full name and any name changes, date and place of birth, current address, occupation, date of emigration and port, **ship name and exact arrival date**, spouse's name and birth date, children's names and birth dates, and two witnesses with addresses. Post-1906 declarations often include a physical description; petitions after 1929 frequently have a photograph.

Search FamilySearch's "United States Naturalization Records" collections and the NARA catalog. Petitions were filed in the court nearest where they lived, which might be a county court rather than a federal district court, so search both. If the arrival date on the petition doesn't match what you found in the manifest, trust the manifest and re-search with the new date.

## Name-spelling pitfalls

The single biggest obstacle. Some specifics:

- **The name on the manifest is the name from the ticket, written at the port of departure**, usually by a clerk who spoke the language. Ellis Island did not change anyone's name. The spelling is often more "correct" than the Americanized version the family later used, so search the original form (Schmidt not Smith, Kowalczyk not Kovalsky).
- **Transcription errors.** Indexers read old handwriting: capital L/S, u/n, and doubled letters get swapped. Try wildcards (Kow*, *walczyk) and Soundex options where available.
- **Given names in the old language.** Giovanni, not John; Chaim, not Hyman; Wojciech may appear as Adalbert.
- **Women under maiden names**, and children listed with a stepfather's surname.
- **Steve Morse's One-Step tools** (stevemorse.org) let you search the Ellis Island database with sounds-like matching, town of origin, and ship-name filters that the official site doesn't expose. When the direct search fails, this is the next stop.

If nothing turns up, search for a sibling or the relative named in family stories; once you find one manifest with the home village on it, everyone else from that family becomes findable.
