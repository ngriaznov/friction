# The First Three Months at a Big Company, After Five Years at a Small One

I joined a company everyone has heard of after five years at a startup where I knew every line of the codebase and every person who could break it. I expected the change to be hard. I didn't expect to spend three months quietly convinced that a hiring mistake had been made and it was only a matter of time before someone noticed.

Here's what that felt like, and what actually made it go away.

## What it felt like

At the startup, I was the person people came to. Here, I couldn't even find the right repository. Every question I asked in a channel felt like evidence. The internal tooling had its own vocabulary, and meetings were full of acronyms I'd nod along to and look up afterward. My first code review came back with eleven comments, most of them about conventions I had no way of knowing.

The specific thought that kept looping was: everyone here got in through the same interview I did, but they all seem to actually know what they're doing.

## What didn't help

Being told "everyone feels this way" didn't help, even though it was true. Reading about imposter syndrome didn't help. Working longer hours to compensate made it worse, because I was tired and still didn't know where the deploy dashboard was.

## What actually helped

**Someone showing me their own onboarding notes.** A teammate who'd joined a year earlier sent me a doc full of questions she'd had in her first month. Half of them were questions I'd been too embarrassed to ask. Seeing that a person I considered competent had once wondered how to run the tests was worth more than any reassurance.

**Shipping something small end to end.** Around week six I fixed a minor bug, wrote the test, got it through review, and watched it deploy. It was trivial. But it was the first time I'd gone through the entire loop by myself, and once I'd done it once, the machinery stopped feeling like a wall.

**Realising the eleven review comments were the norm.** I started reading other people's pull requests, including ones from senior engineers. They also got ten comments. The review culture was thorough for everyone, not a special assessment of me.

**Finding the thing I knew that they didn't.** At the startup I'd done a lot of work on a specific class of performance problem. Around month three, that exact problem showed up in a design discussion, and I had opinions that turned out to be useful. Nobody had hired me for my knowledge of their internal tools. They'd hired me for what I already knew, and I'd been so busy feeling behind that I'd forgotten I had any of it.

**My manager saying, plainly, that I was on track.** Not a pep talk. A one-line message after my first quarterly check-in: "You're where I'd expect a new senior hire to be at three months." Specific, calibrated, and apparently exactly what I needed to hear.

## Where it landed

It didn't vanish. It faded, in the way a loud noise fades once you stop noticing it. Somewhere in month four I answered a question in a channel and realised, a beat later, that I'd been the person who knew. That was the moment it became obvious that the feeling had been about unfamiliarity, not ability. They're easy to confuse when you're in the middle of it.
