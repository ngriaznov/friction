# How I Negotiated Permanent Remote Work with a Manager Who Didn't Want to Say Yes

When I asked my manager about going fully remote, his first reaction was a long pause and then, "I'm not against it, but I have concerns." I've come to appreciate that answer. It was honest, and it gave me a list to work through.

Here are the concerns he raised, and what we did about each one.

## "I'm worried about communication."

This was the big one. He'd had a remote report before who'd gone quiet for days at a time, and the experience had stuck with him.

What I proposed: a short written update at the end of each day for the first two months, posted in our team channel, covering what I'd done and what I was blocked on. I also committed to being reachable during the team's core hours, which we defined explicitly as 10 to 3 in the office's time zone.

The daily updates turned out to be useful enough that two office-based colleagues started doing them too. After two months we dropped the requirement and I kept doing them anyway.

## "How will I know if you're struggling?"

Fair question. In the office he could tell from how I looked at my desk. Remotely, he couldn't.

We agreed to a standing weekly one-on-one that neither of us would cancel, and I promised to say when I was stuck rather than waiting to be asked. I've kept that promise more carefully than I probably would have otherwise, because it was part of the deal.

## "What about mentoring the junior engineers?"

I was doing a lot of informal help at the time, the kind that happens when someone rolls their chair over. He was right that this would change.

I set up two recurring pairing sessions a week with the juniors on the team and kept a "questions welcome" block on my calendar that anyone could grab. The sessions ended up being more structured than the ad hoc help had been, and one of the juniors told me later she'd learned more from them than from the drive-by version.

## "What if it doesn't work?"

He wanted an exit. I wanted one too, honestly, because I'd never worked fully remotely and didn't know if I'd hate it.

We agreed to a three-month trial with a clear review at the end, and we wrote down what "working" meant: on-time delivery on my current project, no drop in review turnaround, and no complaints from the team. At the three-month review, all three held, and we made it permanent without much discussion.

## What I'd tell someone else

Don't argue against the concerns. Ask for them, write them down, and propose something concrete for each one. Make the trial period short and the success criteria measurable. Most of the resistance I hit wasn't about remote work in principle. It was about uncertainty, and specifics are the cure for that.
