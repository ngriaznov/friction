The windowpane test is the most reliable low-tech way to tell whether you've developed enough gluten, and it's worth learning to read properly because "knead for 10 minutes" is a guess that depends on your flour, your hydration and how hard you knead.

## How to do it

Tear off a piece of dough about the size of a walnut. Flour your fingers lightly if the dough is sticky. Hold the piece with both hands and slowly stretch it outward, rotating it as you go, the way you'd stretch a small pizza base. Go slowly; if you yank it, any dough will tear.

## What a developed dough looks like

A properly developed dough will stretch into a thin, translucent membrane that you can see light through, thin enough to make out the shape of your fingers behind it. The edges will stay smooth, and the membrane will hold for several seconds before it eventually gives way. When it does tear, the tear will be a clean split with smooth edges, not a ragged rip.

An underdeveloped dough behaves differently at every stage. It resists stretching and springs back, then tears while it's still opaque and thick. The tear edges look shaggy and jagged, like torn cotton wool, and you'll see individual strands rather than a continuous sheet. If the dough is very early in development, it will just break apart into lumps.

Somewhere in between, you get a dough that stretches thin in the middle but tears at the edges as soon as you try to get the whole piece translucent. That's "almost there", and usually another two or three minutes of kneading does it.

## If it still tears after 10 minutes

Kneading further is often not the answer. Try these first:

1. **Let it rest.** Cover the dough and leave it for 15–20 minutes. Gluten continues to develop with time and hydration alone (this is the whole principle behind autolyse). After the rest, you'll frequently find it passes the test with no extra kneading at all.
2. **Check your flour.** Cake flour or a low-protein all-purpose (below about 10%) may never form a strong windowpane. Use bread flour, or add a tablespoon or two of vital wheat gluten per 500 g of flour.
3. **Check your hydration.** Very stiff, dry doughs (under 60%) resist development and tear. Very wet doughs (over 75%) develop slowly and are better handled with stretch-and-folds every 30 minutes rather than continuous kneading.
4. **Enriched doughs** with lots of butter or sugar take much longer, sometimes 20 minutes in a mixer. Add the fat after the gluten has partly developed.

Finally, don't over-worry. Plenty of good bread is made from dough that only ever reaches "almost there". Overkneading by hand is nearly impossible; a stand mixer on high for a long time can do it, at which point the dough turns slack and sticky and the membrane breaks easily again.
