**Re: Fitting tenons without a shoulder plane – it's fine, use a chisel**

Posted by **oakandash** » Sun Sep 20, 2026 10:47 am

You don't need a shoulder plane. I made furniture for about six years before I owned one, and honestly I still reach for a chisel most of the time because it's faster for the small adjustments a tenon actually needs. Here's how I fit them.

**Cut fat, then sneak up**

Saw the tenon cheeks a hair proud of the line, maybe half a millimetre each side. If you saw to the line and your saw wanders, you're gluing shims. The whole method below relies on having a little material to remove.

**Test the fit before touching anything**

Try the tenon in the mortise. It should not go in. If it goes in easily on the first try, stop and figure out where you lost material, because the fix for a loose tenon is a different (and more annoying) post.

If it starts and binds, look at where it's binding. Rub a bit of pencil graphite on the mortise walls, push the tenon in as far as it'll go, pull it out, and the shiny/smudged spots on the cheeks show you exactly where to pare. This is the single most useful trick in this whole post.

**Paring the cheeks**

Clamp the rail in the vice with the tenon horizontal, cheek up, at a comfortable height. Take a wide, sharp bench chisel – the widest you have, 25 mm or more if the tenon is wide enough. Bevel up, register the back of the chisel flat on the cheek, and pare from the shoulder toward the end of the tenon with the grain. A wide chisel referenced flat on the cheek is basically a very short plane; it won't dig in if you keep it flat.

Two rules:

1. Only pare where the pencil marks are. Don't "even it up" all over.
2. Take shavings, not chips. If you're removing anything you'd call a chip, you'll overshoot.

Work alternate cheeks so the tenon stays centred. If you take everything off one side, the shoulders will end up out of line with the face of the leg.

**Dry assembly, every time**

Pare, wipe off the graphite, re-apply, test again. It feels slow but it's usually only three or four cycles. You want the tenon to go in with firm hand pressure and a mallet tap to seat the last few millimetres. If you need to lean on it, it's still too tight and will split the mortise when the glue swells it. If it drops in under its own weight, that's too loose.

A good check: with the joint assembled dry, lift the whole thing by the rail. The tenon should hold the leg without slipping.

**The shoulders**

The shoulder is what people buy the plane for, but a chisel does that too. Mark a knife line, chop a small V on the waste side, and pare straight down to the knife line with the chisel vertical. If the shoulder isn't closing, it's almost always because the corner where the shoulder meets the cheek isn't clean; undercut it very slightly with the corner of the chisel.

**Shoulder plane, then?**

Buy one when you're doing a lot of wide tenons or long haunches. Until then, a sharp chisel and a pencil.
