# What I Wish I'd Known Before My First Conference Talk

I gave my first talk at a technical conference this year. It went fine. It also could have gone considerably better with about four pieces of advice that nobody gave me and that I now give to everyone who'll listen.

## Slides: fewer words, more time

I built my slides the way I'd build a document, with full sentences and dense bullet lists, and then I read them out loud to the audience. Nobody needs that. If the text is on the slide, people read it faster than you can say it, and then they wait for you to catch up.

What I'd do now: one idea per slide, a few words at most, and a large diagram or code sample where the idea needs one. The slide is a cue for you and a picture for them. The words belong in your mouth.

The other thing: I had 42 slides for a 30-minute slot. I made it to slide 31. Budget about one slide per minute and then cut ten percent for the demo that stalls or the intro that runs long.

## Rehearsal: out loud, standing, timed

I "rehearsed" by clicking through my slides silently and thinking about what I'd say. That is not rehearsing. The first time I actually said the talk out loud was on stage, and I discovered that a sentence which reads fine in my head takes twice as long to say and has a clause in the middle where I always trip.

Do at least three full run-throughs, out loud, standing up, with a timer. Record one and watch it, even though it's excruciating. You'll find the section that's too long, the transition that doesn't work, and the verbal tic you didn't know you had. Mine was "so, yeah." I said it fourteen times.

If you can, do one run-through in front of a real person. A colleague, a partner, anyone who'll tell you where they got lost.

## The demo will fail. Plan for it.

My live demo depended on the conference Wi-Fi. You can guess how that went. I had no backup and spent ninety seconds saying "it usually works" to a room of people who have all said that themselves.

Record a video of the demo working. Put it on the next slide. If the live version works, great; if not, click forward and narrate the recording. Nobody minds.

## Questions: repeat, pause, and it's fine to not know

Three things about the Q&A:

**Repeat the question.** The room can't hear the person in the fourth row, and the recording certainly can't. Repeating also buys you five seconds to think.

**It's fine to say "I don't know."** I got a question about a scaling scenario I'd never tried and I improvised an answer that, on reflection, was probably wrong. "I haven't tested that, but I'd expect X, come find me after" would have been better and more honest.

**Someone will ask a question that's really a comment.** Say "that's a good point," thank them, and move on. You don't owe a debate.

## The part nobody mentions

The best conversations happen in the hallway afterward. People who wouldn't raise their hand in a room of two hundred will walk up and tell you about the problem they're having. Stay near the stage for ten minutes. Bring a way for them to reach you. That part I got right by accident, and it's the reason I'd do it again.
